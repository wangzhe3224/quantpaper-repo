## Abstract

This paper investigates the solar term effect (anomaly) in the China stock market as a supplement to the existing literature on calendar effects. Based on a regression framework, this paper verifies the existence of solar term effects in the Shanghai Index in multiple dimensions: inter-solar-term analysis, full sample analysis at the mean level and risk level, as well as the turn of solar term effect. Several solar terms have been found to cause significant positive and negative values for returns, such as solar terms 1, 3, and 4, and to bring high volatility, such as solar terms 8, 11, and 14. The results are reliable and robust under the Extreme Bound Analysis and various assumptions of the error distribution in the IGARCH model. These findings give readers a new perspective to view calendar effects under the influence of traditional Chinese culture — that solar terms affect the market through affecting investors' mood, expectations, enthusiasm, etc. — which is good evidence for the "Culture bonus hypothesis" proposed by Chen and Chien (2011) and the possible influence of Chinese culture in other Asian markets (Yuan and Gupta, 2014).

**Keywords:** Calendar anomaly (effect), Extreme Bound Analysis, Efficient solar term, Turn of solar term effect

## 1. Introduction

There has been continuous debate regarding the efficiency and regulation of the stock market since Fama (1970) proposed the Efficient Market Hypothesis (EMH). Fama (1970) suggests that all information is fully and rapidly reflected in stock prices and investors are not able to apply any strategy (e.g., technical and fundamental analysis) to earn excess returns based on the historical data of stocks. In other words, stocks are unpredictable with little regulation. However, since the 1980s, behavioral financial economists and scholars have found many anomalies in stock markets that challenge the EMH, among which a famous one is the **calendar anomaly (calendar effect)**. Calendar effect, first discovered in the study of stock returns, refers to a date-related anomaly in which returns are significantly higher or lower than the average at a particular time and investors can benefit through their anticipation on a certain day based on historical regularities. Prevailing calendar effects include the weekend effect, month effect, holiday effect, turn of the month effect, etc. Thaler (1987) and Schwert (2003) provide summaries of abundant introductions of anomalies in early research. It can be seen that the calendar effect is based on various divisions of time and date specifications (e.g., time spots, certain periods of time, seasons, etc.) and that concept is independent from traditional financial and economic factors, which gives us another perspective to seek regularities in the stock market.

In recent years, calendar anomalies in the China stock market have drawn much attention. A number of studies have discovered calendar anomalies based on the Chinese lunar calendar (also called the Chinese farmer calendar), such as the lunar new year effect (Teng and Yang, 2018). These findings have also been investigated in many other Asian stock markets (McGuinness and Harris, 2011; Yang, 2016). Given that the traditional Chinese twenty-four solar terms represent a refined division of annual seasons and climate, the solar term effect (anomaly) is definitely a notable factor in the China stock market. In fact, the chronology of the Chinese twenty-four solar terms is also based on the lunar calendar and is more suitable for studying dates and times with traditional Chinese characteristics. The dates of the twenty-four solar terms are consistent with some of what Gann (2014) mentions in his book.ootnote{Besides Gann's Wheel, Gann's line, etc., Gann also points out a series of dates on which stocks are mutable. Those dates mostly come from dividing a year into 4, 6, 8, 12, etc., equal parts, which is exactly the same as how the Chinese twenty-four solar terms originate. In his book, Gann believes time and date could be an independent factor affecting stocks but he does not associate it with the division of the Chinese twenty-four solar terms.}

In brief, there are 24 refined seasons within a year under the Chinese twenty-four solar terms instead of what we are using internationally today (i.e., only spring, summer, fall, and winter). For the complete introduction of the Chinese twenty-four solar terms, please see Appendix A. The date (chronology) of the Chinese twenty-four solar terms is presented in Table 0.

As a matter of fact, studies of the solar term effect in the China stock market are rare since they are mostly published only in some Chinese platforms and the gap in this research remains large. Ni (2013) explores the solar term effect in the Shanghai Index and Shenzhen Index in early days, finding that there is a positive effect on return on Dahan in both the Shanghai Index and Shenzhen Index, and Dongzhi is also positive if loosening the significance level to 10%, confirming the existence of solar term effects in the China stock market. He also concludes that not all sector indexes show obvious solar term effects, but for those sectors occupying a big proportion in the China stock market, all show a positive effect on Dahan. The drawback of his study, as he also says, is the limit of data, which is only from 2000 to 2012. Wang (2017) takes a similar study with a larger amount of data (1997–2016) of the Shanghai-Shenzhen 300 Index. She finds positive returns on Chunfen and Lichun, which is much more consistent with what we find in this article. Whether solar term effects can be explained by Holiday Effect and Festival Effect was also tested in several other markets of China and countries abroad. On the other hand, Zhang, Ou and Xu (2018) study the relationship between the solar term effect and holiday effect, finding that there is a positive return during the Spring Festival, Qingming, and Dongzhi. Worth mentioning is that our team (Zhou, Li and Wang, 2021) conducts a brand-new study on the relationship between stock index turning points (reversals) and Chinese twenty-four solar terms, finding that overall, the trend of stock indices is more likely to reverse around solar terms.

Generally speaking, current studies of solar terms are limited and lack systematicness in sufficiently verifying solar term effects. This paper studies the solar term effect in the Shanghai Index based on a regression framework using a large span of sample from 1995 to 2022. The main contribution of this paper to the literature is that we verify solar term effects from multiple dimensions: inter-solar-term, full sample mean level and full sample risk level, and by various robust tests. We systematically analyze abundant categories of possible solar term effects in a large span of sample and further explain the relationship between existing calendar effects, traditional Chinese culture, and the solar term effect in an effort to figure out the potential factors that shape the solar term effect in the China stock market.

In the inter-solar-term analysis, apart from using a purely dummy regression model, we also introduce the **Extreme Bound Analysis (EBA)** for a more reliable robust test since that method is more stringent. We find that several solar terms at the beginning of a year have significant positive and negative returns, such as solar terms 1, 3, and 4. Some of them passed the EBA robust test. In the full sample analysis, we use an AR(1)-IGARCH(1,1) model to capture return features at the mean level and risk level correspondingly. Involving non-solar-term days (henceforth, "normal days") and solar-term days, we discover remarkable features of a couple of solar terms having significant value and contribution based on multiple error assumptions in an effort to verify the strength (or called efficiency) of each solar term. The result of the turn of solar term effect is also notable. Those significant solar terms in inter-solar-term analysis and full sample analysis are highly consistent (mostly early in the year such as solar terms 1, 2, 3, and 4). Therefore, the existing solar term effect is confirmed to be robust and stable in all cases under multi-dimensional analysis. The result reveals the potential impact of Chinese culture and investors' mood as a sort of "Early year effect" because the Spring Festival and Spring solar terms happen at that period. This interesting result also supports the previous studies of the Month effect and January effect as the first two solar terms are in January. It also verifies the impact of the existing Chinese lunar new year (Spring Festival) effect as solar term 3 (Lichun) is always closely before and after the Spring Festival. This can be explained by the "Culture bonus hypothesis" proposed by Chen and Chien (2011). The solar term effect is a new perspective to understand the influence that Chinese investors get more hopes and expectations since a fresh new year, as well as the active trading and impetuousness brought by the rising temperature and revival of life in warm seasons, which is an ingrained influence on Chinese people.

To sum up, this paper answers: 1) which solar terms are significant in inter-solar-term comparison; 2) which solar terms are significant out of the full sample both at the mean level and risk (volatility) level; 3) the turn of solar term effect at the risk level; and 4) the logic of how solar term effects may shape in the China stock market. The remainder of this paper is as follows: Section 2 introduces our data and methodology; Section 3 presents the empirical results; Section 4 is the conclusions; and Section 5 is the discussions.

## 2. Data and Methodology

The data we use in this study is the daily return of the **Shanghai Index**ootnote{Shanghai Index (Shanghai Securities Composite Index) is the earliest and the most influential stock index in China. It is a weighted composite index covering a major part of blue-chip stocks and dominant sectors in the China market such as banks, securities and insurance, conglomerates, state-owned enterprises, energy, etc. As it contains thousands of stocks, it is relatively smooth and steady in trend compared with other technology indexes. Usually, the whole market goes up when the Shanghai Index has a significant rise. The Shanghai Index is used as a wind indicator for the overall heat and sentiment of the market. Thus, the Shanghai Index is suitable to be a proxy for the China stock market.} in China (code: 000001) from the first trading day in 1995 (Jan 1, 1995) to the last trading day in 2022 (Dec 30, 2022). That is 28 entire years and there are totally $24 	imes 28 = 672$ solar terms in our dataset (some of the solar terms are not on trading days, so we exclude them). Given that the daily price series of the Shanghai Index is not stationary, we simply study its daily return series throughout this paper. The data is easy and free to obtain from most securities websites.ootnote{The data is open and free to download from securities websites like https://www.eastmoney.com and http://data.10jqka.com.cn, or from leading dataset websites: https://www.gtarsc.com/, and the WIND database in China.}

A predominant and reasonable way to examine calendar effects is to apply the regression model with several dummy variables corresponding to the days we refer to. Note that the study on solar term effects (anomalies) is slightly different from those on weekend effects or month effects. Monday to Friday occupy all trading days in a week and January to December cover all the trading months within a year. However, a trading day is not necessarily a solar term day or another. Solar terms follow the Chinese lunar calendar, so we need to distinguish solar-term days and non-solar-term days before making up the model.

### 2.1. Inter-Solar-Term Analysis

In this section, we solely select solar-term days from our sample and study purely on the features and comparison among twenty-four solar terms. In such a background, daily returns have the following dummy regression model:

$$r_t = \mu + \sum_{i=1}^{24} eta_i D_{i,t} + arepsilon_t 	ag{1}$$

where $r_t$ denotes the daily return, $D_{i,t}$ denotes the $i$-th solar term dummy variable such that $D_{i,t} = 1$ if solar term No. $i$ falls on that day, otherwise $D_{i,t} = 0$.

It is common sense that equation (1) cannot be estimated due to perfect collinearity of the full use of dummy variables, which is called the **dummy variable trap** (i.e., $\sum_{i=1}^{24} D_{i,t} = 1$). This means that in a regression model with an intercept, the intercept and 24 terms are linearly related. In other words, after setting 24 dummy variables for 24 solar terms correspondingly and when all dummy variables equal zero, there is no 25th solar term for the intercept to explain. In order to solve the problem, some restrictions are needed on how to specify the dummy variables. A widely-used approach is the dummy code by deleting one dummy variable from all. In other words, we need to specify $n-1$ dummy variables if there are $n$ cases in total. In this study, we finally specify 23 dummy variables as there are 24 solar terms in total. The unselected (deleted) one is called the **reference variable** (reference solar term) which serves as the constant term in the regression. Therefore, equation (1) is modified to:

$$r_t = \mu + \sum_{\substack{i=1 \ i 
eq i^*}}^{24} eta_i D_{i,t} + arepsilon_t 	ag{2}$$

where $st^*$ is the reference solar term. The feature of $st^*$ is reflected on $\mu$:

$$E(r_t \mid D_{i,t} = 1) = \mu + eta_i \quad (i 
eq i^*) 	ag{3}$$

$$E(r_t \mid D_{i^*,t} = 1) = \mu 	ag{4}$$

$eta_i$ is the relative contribution on return compared with the reference solar term, whereas $\mu$ is the absolute contribution to return. Now, another issue is raised: how do we choose the reference term? Theoretically, the choice of picking the reference term is arbitrary, but we simplify it and make the choice more efficient according to the statistical features of solar terms, which we analyze in detail in the following chapter.

Worth mentioning is that such a dummy variable trap will not exist in a regression model without an intercept as in equation (1'):

$$r_t = \sum_{i=1}^{24} eta_i D_{i,t} + arepsilon_t 	ag{1'}$$

where none of the dummy variables is required to be excluded from the model and picking a reference term is also no longer needed. However, predominant regression models always contain an intercept as the estimation for R-square and interpretation for coefficients may not be accessible in a model without an intercept, causing extra problems (see Gerald, 1977; Jobson, 1982; Hawkins, 1980). Many statistical software packages always introduce an intercept by default.

In addition, another way to examine the robustness of estimated coefficients in a regression is the **Extreme Bound Analysis (EBA)** (Leamer, 1983, 1985). EBA suggests a new way to assess the error of coefficients in a panel data model since heteroskedasticity leads to consistent but inefficient least squares estimates in a linear regression model. EBA may result in high error for coefficients, and the coefficient is regarded as robust under EBA when it falls within the upper and lower bound of EBA without changing its sign; otherwise, it is fragile. EBA is regarded as a very stringent criterion for robustness (Salai-Martin, 1997). As a result, in applications it is generally the case that most (if not all) of the candidate regression variables are declared fragile. Hence, if a variable is regarded as robust under EBA, it is quite likely to be cataloged as robust with other methods dealing with model uncertainty.

In a traditional regression model $y = Xeta + arepsilon$, EBA focuses on estimating the covariance matrix for the coefficient $eta$. Finally, we have the lower and upper bound of each coefficient $\hat{eta}_i$, which are $\hat{eta}_i - u_i\sigma_i$ and $\hat{eta}_i + u_i\sigma_i$.

Each $\sigma_i$ is calculated from the EBA covariance matrix. There are a series of estimators for it but the most recommended is the **HC3 estimator** (Winkelried and Iberico, 2018). For details on other estimators (e.g., HC1 and HC2), see Francisco et al. (2006) and MacKinnon (1985). Winkelried and Iberico (2018) apply the EBA method on the weekend effect by simplifying the HC3 estimator into a more clear and concise form which has identical results as the original one in our study. The HC3 covariance matrix Winkelried and Iberico (2018) adopt is constructed as follows:

$$V_{HC3} = (X'X)^{-1} X' 	ext{diag}(e_j^2 / (1-h_j)^2) X (X'X)^{-1} 	ag{5}$$

where $H = X(X'X)^{-1}X'$ and $h_1, h_2, \ldots, h_n$ are the diagonal elements of $H$. By taking the square root of the diagonal elements of $V_{HC3}$, we can finally obtain the standard error of each estimated coefficient under EBA:

$$\sigma_i = \sqrt{V_{HC3,ii}} 	ag{6}$$

Therefore, EBA serves as a supplementary test for the robustness of the dummy regression.

### 2.2. Full Sample Analysis

The analysis in Section 2.1 only focuses on the 24 solar terms but does not consider other non-solar term days (henceforth "normal days"). We are going to extend it to the full sample range in this section. Based on previous works, we know that financial series always accompany autocorrelation and heteroscedasticity. To address the problem above, many papers combine the time series framework with dummy regression (e.g., Halil and Berument, 2003; Sabri et al., 2017). We thus include an AR(1) process to examine the solar term anomaly in the return (mean level). The process is as follows:

$$r_t = \mu + \sum_{i=1}^{24} eta_i D_{i,t} + ho r_{t-1} + arepsilon_t 	ag{7}$$

where the symbols remain the same as mentioned in Section 2.1, $r_{t-1}$ denotes the return of the last trading day, and $ho$ is its coefficient. In equation (7), there are 25 types of day: 24 solar term days plus normal days. Therefore, it will not cause a dummy variable trap to specify all 24 solar terms. When all dummy variables equal zero, it refers to a normal day. The advantage is that it makes it possible for all solar terms to be interpreted equally, and the constant $\mu$ contains the information of normal days. The lagged term of return also contributes to the accuracy of the model.

Besides, we also want to examine the solar term anomaly at the risk level (i.e., variance or volatility). Therefore, we allow variances of errors to be time-varying in equation (7) through including a conditional heteroscedasticity equation that captures the risk. The error terms now have a mean of zero and a time-varying variance of $h_t$. Engle (1982) proposes the ARCH($q$) model first, which allows the squared error to be affected by its lagged terms. Many other derivations of the ARCH model are further developed by scholars and researchers (Baillie and DeGennaro, 1990). It can be easily verified that the sample of the Shanghai Index is more likely to meet the condition of the **IGARCH(1,1) model**ootnote{The parameter $lpha + eta$ is so close to unity. It is reasonable to say that there is a unit root in that model, thus we use IGARCH(1,1) instead of traditional GARCH(1,1). Related works see Thomas and Stărică (2004); Ling (2007).} instead of the traditional GARCH(1,1) model. We make the variance model as follows:

$$h_t = \omega + \sum_{i=1}^{24} \gamma_i D_{i,t} + lpha h_{t-1}^2 + eta arepsilon_{t-1}^2 	ag{8}$$
