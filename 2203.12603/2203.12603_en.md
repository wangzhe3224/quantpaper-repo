## Abstract

This paper investigates the solar term effect (anomaly) in the China stock market as a supplement to the existing literature on calendar effects. Based on a regression framework, this paper verifies the existence of solar term effects in the Shanghai Index in multiple dimensions: inter-solar-term analysis, full sample analysis at the mean level and risk level, as well as the turn of solar term effect. Several solar terms have been found to cause significant positive and negative values for returns, such as solar terms 1, 3, and 4, and to bring high volatility, such as solar terms 8, 11, and 14. The results are reliable and robust under the Extreme Bound Analysis and various assumptions of the error distribution in the IGARCH model. These findings give readers a new perspective to view calendar effects under the influence of traditional Chinese culture — that solar terms affect the market through affecting investors' mood, expectations, enthusiasm, etc. — which is good evidence for the "Culture bonus hypothesis" proposed by Chen and Chien (2011) and the possible influence of Chinese culture in other Asian markets (Yuan and Gupta, 2014).

**Keywords:** Calendar anomaly (effect), Extreme Bound Analysis, Efficient solar term, Turn of solar term effect

## 1. Introduction

There has been continuous debate regarding the efficiency and regulation of the stock market since Fama (1970) proposed the Efficient Market Hypothesis (EMH). Fama (1970) suggests that all information is fully and rapidly reflected in stock prices and investors are not able to apply any strategy (e.g., technical and fundamental analysis) to earn excess returns based on the historical data of stocks. In other words, stocks are unpredictable with little regulation. However, since the 1980s, behavioral financial economists and scholars have found many anomalies in stock markets that challenge the EMH, among which a famous one is the **calendar anomaly (calendar effect)**. Calendar effect, first discovered in the study of stock returns, refers to a date-related anomaly in which returns are significantly higher or lower than the average at a particular time and investors can benefit through their anticipation on a certain day based on historical regularities. Prevailing calendar effects include the weekend effect, month effect, holiday effect, turn of the month effect, etc. Thaler (1987) and Schwert (2003) provide summaries of abundant introductions of anomalies in early research. It can be seen that the calendar effect is based on various divisions of time and date specifications (e.g., time spots, certain periods of time, seasons, etc.) and that concept is independent from traditional financial and economic factors, which gives us another perspective to seek regularities in the stock market.

In recent years, calendar anomalies in the China stock market have drawn much attention. A number of studies have discovered calendar anomalies based on the Chinese lunar calendar (also called the Chinese farmer calendar), such as the lunar new year effect (Teng and Yang, 2018). These findings have also been investigated in many other Asian stock markets (McGuinness and Harris, 2011; Yang, 2016). Given that the traditional Chinese twenty-four solar terms represent a refined division of annual seasons and climate, the solar term effect (anomaly) is definitely a notable factor in the China stock market. In fact, the chronology of the Chinese twenty-four solar terms is also based on the lunar calendar and is more suitable for studying dates and times with traditional Chinese characteristics. The dates of the twenty-four solar terms are consistent with some of what Gann (2014) mentions in his book.\footnote{Besides Gann's Wheel, Gann's line, etc., Gann also points out a series of dates on which stocks are mutable. Those dates mostly come from dividing a year into 4, 6, 8, 12, etc., equal parts, which is exactly the same as how the Chinese twenty-four solar terms originate. In his book, Gann believes time and date could be an independent factor affecting stocks but he does not associate it with the division of the Chinese twenty-four solar terms.}

In brief, there are 24 refined seasons within a year under the Chinese twenty-four solar terms instead of what we are using internationally today (i.e., only spring, summer, fall, and winter). For the complete introduction of the Chinese twenty-four solar terms, please see Appendix A. The date (chronology) of the Chinese twenty-four solar terms is presented in Table 0.

As a matter of fact, studies of the solar term effect in the China stock market are rare since they are mostly published only in some Chinese platforms and the gap in this research remains large. Ni (2013) explores the solar term effect in the Shanghai Index and Shenzhen Index in early days, finding that there is a positive effect on return on Dahan in both the Shanghai Index and Shenzhen Index, and Dongzhi is also positive if loosening the significance level to 10%, confirming the existence of solar term effects in the China stock market. He also concludes that not all sector indexes show obvious solar term effects, but for those sectors occupying a big proportion in the China stock market, all show a positive effect on Dahan. The drawback of his study, as he also says, is the limit of data, which is only from 2000 to 2012. Wang (2017) takes a similar study with a larger amount of data (1997–2016) of the Shanghai-Shenzhen 300 Index. She finds positive returns on Chunfen and Lichun, which is much more consistent with what we find in this article. Whether solar term effects can be explained by Holiday Effect and Festival Effect was also tested in several other markets of China and countries abroad. On the other hand, Zhang, Ou and Xu (2018) study the relationship between the solar term effect and holiday effect, finding that there is a positive return during the Spring Festival, Qingming, and Dongzhi. Worth mentioning is that our team (Zhou, Li and Wang, 2021) conducts a brand-new study on the relationship between stock index turning points (reversals) and Chinese twenty-four solar terms, finding that overall, the trend of stock indices is more likely to reverse around solar terms.

Generally speaking, current studies of solar terms are limited and lack systematicness in sufficiently verifying solar term effects. This paper studies the solar term effect in the Shanghai Index based on a regression framework using a large span of sample from 1995 to 2022. The main contribution of this paper to the literature is that we verify solar term effects from multiple dimensions: inter-solar-term, full sample mean level and full sample risk level, and by various robust tests. We systematically analyze abundant categories of possible solar term effects in a large span of sample and further explain the relationship between existing calendar effects, traditional Chinese culture, and the solar term effect in an effort to figure out the potential factors that shape the solar term effect in the China stock market.

In the inter-solar-term analysis, apart from using a purely dummy regression model, we also introduce the **Extreme Bound Analysis (EBA)** for a more reliable robust test since that method is more stringent. We find that several solar terms at the beginning of a year have significant positive and negative returns, such as solar terms 1, 3, and 4. Some of them passed the EBA robust test. In the full sample analysis, we use an AR(1)-IGARCH(1,1) model to capture return features at the mean level and risk level correspondingly. Involving non-solar-term days (henceforth, "normal days") and solar-term days, we discover remarkable features of a couple of solar terms having significant value and contribution based on multiple error assumptions in an effort to verify the strength (or called efficiency) of each solar term. The result of the turn of solar term effect is also notable. Those significant solar terms in inter-solar-term analysis and full sample analysis are highly consistent (mostly early in the year such as solar terms 1, 2, 3, and 4). Therefore, the existing solar term effect is confirmed to be robust and stable in all cases under multi-dimensional analysis. The result reveals the potential impact of Chinese culture and investors' mood as a sort of "Early year effect" because the Spring Festival and Spring solar terms happen at that period. This interesting result also supports the previous studies of the Month effect and January effect as the first two solar terms are in January. It also verifies the impact of the existing Chinese lunar new year (Spring Festival) effect as solar term 3 (Lichun) is always closely before and after the Spring Festival. This can be explained by the "Culture bonus hypothesis" proposed by Chen and Chien (2011). The solar term effect is a new perspective to understand the influence that Chinese investors get more hopes and expectations since a fresh new year, as well as the active trading and impetuousness brought by the rising temperature and revival of life in warm seasons, which is an ingrained influence on Chinese people.

To sum up, this paper answers: 1) which solar terms are significant in inter-solar-term comparison; 2) which solar terms are significant out of the full sample both at the mean level and risk (volatility) level; 3) the turn of solar term effect at the risk level; and 4) the logic of how solar term effects may shape in the China stock market. The remainder of this paper is as follows: Section 2 introduces our data and methodology; Section 3 presents the empirical results; Section 4 is the conclusions; and Section 5 is the discussions.

## 2. Data and Methodology

The data we use in this study is the daily return of the **Shanghai Index**\footnote{Shanghai Index (Shanghai Securities Composite Index) is the earliest and the most influential stock index in China. It is a weighted composite index covering a major part of blue-chip stocks and dominant sectors in the China market such as banks, securities and insurance, conglomerates, state-owned enterprises, energy, etc. As it contains thousands of stocks, it is relatively smooth and steady in trend compared with other technology indexes. Usually, the whole market goes up when the Shanghai Index has a significant rise. The Shanghai Index is used as a wind indicator for the overall heat and sentiment of the market. Thus, the Shanghai Index is suitable to be a proxy for the China stock market.} in China (code: 000001) from the first trading day in 1995 (Jan 1, 1995) to the last trading day in 2022 (Dec 30, 2022). That is 28 entire years and there are totally $24 \times 28 = 672$ solar terms in our dataset (some of the solar terms are not on trading days, so we exclude them). Given that the daily price series of the Shanghai Index is not stationary, we simply study its daily return series throughout this paper. The data is easy and free to obtain from most securities websites.\footnote{The data is open and free to download from securities websites like https://www.eastmoney.com and http://data.10jqka.com.cn, or from leading dataset websites: https://www.gtarsc.com/, and the WIND database in China.}

A predominant and reasonable way to examine calendar effects is to apply the regression model with several dummy variables corresponding to the days we refer to. Note that the study on solar term effects (anomalies) is slightly different from those on weekend effects or month effects. Monday to Friday occupy all trading days in a week and January to December cover all the trading months within a year. However, a trading day is not necessarily a solar term day or another. Solar terms follow the Chinese lunar calendar, so we need to distinguish solar-term days and non-solar-term days before making up the model.

### 2.1. Inter-Solar-Term Analysis

In this section, we solely select solar-term days from our sample and study purely on the features and comparison among twenty-four solar terms. In such a background, daily returns have the following dummy regression model:

$$r_t = \mu + \sum_{i=1}^{24} \beta_i D_{i,t} + \varepsilon_t \tag{1}$$

where $r_t$ denotes the daily return, $D_{i,t}$ denotes the $i$-th solar term dummy variable such that $D_{i,t} = 1$ if solar term No. $i$ falls on that day, otherwise $D_{i,t} = 0$.

It is common sense that equation (1) cannot be estimated due to perfect collinearity of the full use of dummy variables, which is called the **dummy variable trap** (i.e., $\sum_{i=1}^{24} D_{i,t} = 1$). This means that in a regression model with an intercept, the intercept and 24 terms are linearly related. In other words, after setting 24 dummy variables for 24 solar terms correspondingly and when all dummy variables equal zero, there is no 25th solar term for the intercept to explain. In order to solve the problem, some restrictions are needed on how to specify the dummy variables. A widely-used approach is the dummy code by deleting one dummy variable from all. In other words, we need to specify $n-1$ dummy variables if there are $n$ cases in total. In this study, we finally specify 23 dummy variables as there are 24 solar terms in total. The unselected (deleted) one is called the **reference variable** (reference solar term) which serves as the constant term in the regression. Therefore, equation (1) is modified to:

$$r_t = \mu + \sum_{\substack{i=1 \\ i \neq i^*}}^{24} \beta_i D_{i,t} + \varepsilon_t \tag{2}$$

where $st^*$ is the reference solar term. The feature of $st^*$ is reflected on $\mu$:

$$E(r_t \mid D_{i,t} = 1) = \mu + \beta_i \quad (i \neq i^*) \tag{3}$$

$$E(r_t \mid D_{i^*,t} = 1) = \mu \tag{4}$$

$\beta_i$ is the relative contribution on return compared with the reference solar term, whereas $\mu$ is the absolute contribution to return. Now, another issue is raised: how do we choose the reference term? Theoretically, the choice of picking the reference term is arbitrary, but we simplify it and make the choice more efficient according to the statistical features of solar terms, which we analyze in detail in the following chapter.

Worth mentioning is that such a dummy variable trap will not exist in a regression model without an intercept as in equation (1'):

$$r_t = \sum_{i=1}^{24} \beta_i D_{i,t} + \varepsilon_t \tag{1'}$$

where none of the dummy variables is required to be excluded from the model and picking a reference term is also no longer needed. However, predominant regression models always contain an intercept as the estimation for R-square and interpretation for coefficients may not be accessible in a model without an intercept, causing extra problems (see Gerald, 1977; Jobson, 1982; Hawkins, 1980). Many statistical software packages always introduce an intercept by default.

In addition, another way to examine the robustness of estimated coefficients in a regression is the **Extreme Bound Analysis (EBA)** (Leamer, 1983, 1985). EBA suggests a new way to assess the error of coefficients in a panel data model since heteroskedasticity leads to consistent but inefficient least squares estimates in a linear regression model. EBA may result in high error for coefficients, and the coefficient is regarded as robust under EBA when it falls within the upper and lower bound of EBA without changing its sign; otherwise, it is fragile. EBA is regarded as a very stringent criterion for robustness (Salai-Martin, 1997). As a result, in applications it is generally the case that most (if not all) of the candidate regression variables are declared fragile. Hence, if a variable is regarded as robust under EBA, it is quite likely to be cataloged as robust with other methods dealing with model uncertainty.

In a traditional regression model $y = X\beta + \varepsilon$, EBA focuses on estimating the covariance matrix for the coefficient $\beta$. Finally, we have the lower and upper bound of each coefficient $\hat{\beta}_i$, which are $\hat{\beta}_i - u_i\sigma_i$ and $\hat{\beta}_i + u_i\sigma_i$.

Each $\sigma_i$ is calculated from the EBA covariance matrix. There are a series of estimators for it but the most recommended is the **HC3 estimator** (Winkelried and Iberico, 2018). For details on other estimators (e.g., HC1 and HC2), see Francisco et al. (2006) and MacKinnon (1985). Winkelried and Iberico (2018) apply the EBA method on the weekend effect by simplifying the HC3 estimator into a more clear and concise form which has identical results as the original one in our study. The HC3 covariance matrix Winkelried and Iberico (2018) adopt is constructed as follows:

$$V_{HC3} = (X'X)^{-1} X' \text{diag}(e_j^2 / (1-h_j)^2) X (X'X)^{-1} \tag{5}$$

where $H = X(X'X)^{-1}X'$ and $h_1, h_2, \ldots, h_n$ are the diagonal elements of $H$. By taking the square root of the diagonal elements of $V_{HC3}$, we can finally obtain the standard error of each estimated coefficient under EBA:

$$\sigma_i = \sqrt{V_{HC3,ii}} \tag{6}$$

Therefore, EBA serves as a supplementary test for the robustness of the dummy regression.

### 2.2. Full Sample Analysis

The analysis in Section 2.1 only focuses on the 24 solar terms but does not consider other non-solar term days (henceforth "normal days"). We are going to extend it to the full sample range in this section. Based on previous works, we know that financial series always accompany autocorrelation and heteroscedasticity. To address the problem above, many papers combine the time series framework with dummy regression (e.g., Halil and Berument, 2003; Sabri et al., 2017). We thus include an AR(1) process to examine the solar term anomaly in the return (mean level). The process is as follows:

$$r_t = \mu + \sum_{i=1}^{24} \beta_i D_{i,t} + \rho r_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t \tag{7}$$

where the symbols remain the same as mentioned in Section 2.1, $r_{t-1}$ denotes the return of the last trading day, and $\rho$ is its coefficient. In equation (7), there are 25 types of day: 24 solar term days plus normal days. Therefore, it will not cause a dummy variable trap to specify all 24 solar terms. When all dummy variables equal zero, it refers to a normal day. The advantage is that it makes it possible for all solar terms to be interpreted equally, and the constant $\mu$ contains the information of normal days. The lagged term of return also contributes to the accuracy of the model.

Besides, we also want to examine the solar term anomaly at the risk level (i.e., variance or volatility). Therefore, we allow variances of errors to be time-varying in equation (7) through including a conditional heteroscedasticity equation that captures the risk. The error terms now have a mean of zero and a time-varying variance of $h_t$. Engle (1982) proposes the ARCH($q$) model first, which allows the squared error to be affected by its lagged terms. Many other derivations of the ARCH model are further developed by scholars and researchers (Baillie and DeGennaro, 1990). It can be easily verified that the sample of the Shanghai Index is more likely to meet the condition of the **IGARCH(1,1) model**\footnote{The parameter $\alpha + \beta$ is so close to unity. It is reasonable to say that there is a unit root in that model, thus we use IGARCH(1,1) instead of traditional GARCH(1,1). Related works see Thomas and Stărică (2004); Ling (2007).} instead of the traditional GARCH(1,1) model. We make the variance model as follows:

$$h_t = \omega + \sum_{i=1}^{24} \gamma_i D_{i,t} + \alpha h_{t-1}^2 + \beta \varepsilon_{t-1}^2 \tag{8}$$

where IGARCH(1,1) requests that $\alpha + \beta = 1$. Note that we do not know the actual distribution of the error terms, so we estimate the IGARCH equation under multiple distribution assumptions in order to test the robustness of solar terms, as the ordinary Normal Distribution assumption (i.e., $\varepsilon_t \sim N(0, h_t^2)$) for error terms in financial series may be too loose. Therefore, two more distribution assumptions are considered in the estimation: **Student-t distribution** and **Generalized Error Distribution (GED)**.\footnote{The density of GED is $f(x) = \frac{v \exp(-\frac{1}{2}|x/\lambda|^v)}{\lambda 2^{(1+1/v)}\Gamma(1/v)}$, where $\Gamma(\cdot)$ is the gamma function, $v > 0$, $\lambda$ is the constant. When $v$ equals 2 and $\lambda$ equals 1, it turns out to be the standard Normal Distribution. See Hamilton (1994) for details.} Nelson (1991) proposes using GED to capture the fat tails feature observed in the distribution of financial time series. Brooks and Persand (2001) and Doyle and Chen (2009) analyze the sensitivity to the choice of error distribution assumptions.

## 4. Conclusions

This paper investigates the existing solar term effects (anomalies) in the China stock market based on the Shanghai Index from 1995 to 2022. By applying a regression framework and EBA method, this paper mainly verifies the effect from three dimensions: inter-solar-term analysis, full sample analysis both at the mean and risk level under multiple error distribution assumptions, as well as the turn of solar terms effect. The result is remarkable: not all solar terms are efficient but we still find a couple of them significant in all cases, confirming the robustness and efficiency of some particular solar terms. This gives investors in the China stock market a clue and reference to gain excess profit.

In the inter-solar-term analysis, among 24 solar terms, solar terms 1, 3, and 13 turn out to be significantly positive (at 5%, 5%, and 10% level) to daily return, whereas solar term 4 is significantly negative at the 10% level. Solar term 2 is another infrequent one having a relatively high negative contribution to return but failing to pass the robust test. Under EBA, only solar terms 1 and 3 pass the robust test at the 5% and 10% level. This shows that not all solar terms are efficient and the power of them is different. Eventually, solar terms 1 and 3 turn out to be the strongest efficient ones, solar term 4 is efficient but less strong, and solar term 2 is not efficient under inter-solar-term analysis but rather a potential candidate in further sections.

In the full sample analysis, we use an AR(1)-IGARCH(1,1) model to extend the study involving both solar term days and non-solar-term days (i.e., normal days) to further verify the universality of the solar term effect. At the mean level, solar terms 1, 3, 4, and 13 are efficient in causing significant contribution to daily return. Still, solar terms 1, 3, and 13 are positive and solar term 4 is negative, which is exactly consistent with the findings in the inter-solar-term analysis. At the risk level (volatility), we run the IGARCH model under three different error distribution assumptions: Normal distribution, Student-t distribution, and Generalized Error Distribution. Under Normal distribution, solar terms 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 7, 8, 14, and 19 are significant in causing high volatility to the return, and under Student-t distribution, the result only leaves solar terms 1, 2, 4, 8, 14, and 19. When it comes to the strictest assumption — GED distribution — only solar terms 1, 2, 4, and 14 are significant. With the strictness of assumptions increasing, significant solar terms reduce. The result is a bit different from the analysis before but still noticing some of the familiar solar terms such as solar terms 1, 2, 3, and 4.

We also investigate the turn of solar terms effect at the risk level based on the same model. We set the range for one day and two days respectively and finally verified that solar terms 8 and 11 are the strongest and most robust, and solar term 14 is the second in causing high volatility within their duration.

From multiple dimensions and multiple tests, we confirm the existence, robustness, and reliability of solar term effects (anomalies) in the China stock market. This helps investors gain excess profit in real trading as well as serves as a good supplementary to understand investors' behavior academically.

## 5. Discussions

Despite the solar term effect (anomaly) being a brand-new discovery in the market, we are still able to find some potential connections and reasonable logic between the solar term effect and prevailing calendar effects from our results in this paper.

Firstly, the solar term effect is robust and reliable in multiple dimensions (i.e., inter-solar-term and full sample), which illustrates that it is a sort of "financial-independent" factor no matter comparing among themselves or comparing with normal days. In other words, efficient solar terms are always efficient in most cases and levels. Most of the significant solar terms above are in the first half of the year (i.e., having small order numbers) and especially gather on the first couple of terms in the year at the mean level. At the risk level, the result expands. Worth noticing is that the holiday duration of the Chinese lunar new year (i.e., the Spring Festival) is 7 days, always right before, right after, or simply overlapping solar term 3. Solar term 3 (see in Appendix A) has an equal meaning to the lunar new year as they both stand for a fresh beginning to the coming year and warm days. In some years, solar term 3 is just the last (first) trading days before (after) the lunar new year holiday, which gives us a clue that the significant positive effect on solar term 3 could be explained as another form of Chinese Lunar New Year (CLNY) effect or holiday effect in China, which is analogous to the conclusions of McGuinness and Harris (2011). Besides, Yuan and Gupta (2014) report this phenomenon in other Asian markets, which implies that the effect may not only be in China but also in other Asian countries having a profound influence from traditional Chinese culture.

As we conclude, the first several solar terms show significant effects both at the mean level and risk level, which also consists with the **"Culture bonus hypothesis"** proposed by Chen and Chien (2011). They suggest that under Chinese tradition, employees are rewarded with a generous bonus before the Lunar New Year, most often paid in January, the period of time overlapping the first several solar terms. They point out that it is analogous to the concept of house money, enhances the propensity to bear increased levels of risk, which in turn stimulates the demand for higher risk securities, particularly in a market that is mainly dominated by individual investors, as in Taiwan (also affected by Chinese culture even more than the mainland). This may also help to explain the high volatility and positive effect of solar term 1 because solar term 1 is right after the international new year holiday. Future study around solar term 1 and its duration may help better reveal the January effect in China.

Following cultural impact, the solar term effect may also be well-explained from the perspective of investor mood. With the coming new year (i.e., solar terms 1 and 2 after the international new year and solar term 3 after the lunar new year), people get more hopes and enthusiasm to wish for a better year. The findings in this paper affirm the narrative of Teng and Yang (2018) who conclude that using emotion proxies provided by the literature, investors' good mood toward the festivities can help explain the CLNY effect. While the effect remains strong for the long term, the CLNY effect gradually subsides after the opening up of A- and B-share markets. To the extent that foreign investors are less affected by the CLNY, the increased foreign participation in Chinese stock markets may help explain the diminishing CLNY effect. In addition, with the rise of temperature in the beginning of a year, investors' mood becomes active. In this regard, solar terms 13 and 14 are in the dog days of the year causing human fluctuation in mood. Our implication is in line with Kang et al. (2009) who find that the weather effect has a strong influence on the volatility of both A- and B-share returns in the China market and the weather effect on volatility is explained by the openness of the B-share market, indicating that the market openness to domestic investors results in the weather effect. Liu (2013) also verifies the impact on investors' mood by weather and climate in a similar way.

Another interesting coincidence is that Wang, Lin and Chen (2010) figure out a lunar cycle effect. Their results imply that the moon affects individual mood and thinking, and leads to stock market changes. They investigate new moon and full moon effects, finding significant returns in certain periods. New moon falls usually on the first day of a lunar month and it is a crescent moon. Full moon is the 15th day of each lunar month, and new-full-new moon composes a month cycle in the Chinese lunar calendar. That's almost the analogue date of the monthly solar term. Solar terms fall not necessarily the same day as new and full moon but the error is within one day. Solar terms have the same 15+15-day cycle as the moon.

The solar term effect in this paper is a new perspective to view calendar effects under the influence of traditional Chinese culture. In general, solar terms in particular periods and seasons affect investors' mood and enthusiasm in trade and further form anomalies, which shows a contradiction to the EMH, making it possible for other studies and efforts in seeking regularities in the stock market through price, volume, and time (Sullivan, Timmermann and White, 2001; Xiong et al., 2018).

## Appendix A: Introduction of Chinese Twenty-Four Solar Terms

The Chinese twenty-four solar terms (known as "Jie Qi" in Chinese) is a very important part of ancient Chinese culture and guidance on agricultural activities even today (Chen, Li and Li, 2023). In traditional Chinese culture, there are roughly 360 days in a year divided into 24 equal periods, each led by a solar term, and there are around 15 days during each solar term period on average. Solar terms come one after another as a time duration and each solar term dominates the following 14 days (i.e., altogether 15 days on average). Every solar term has a different name and a unique meaning showing the change and feature of the seasons at different levels. Today's twenty-four solar terms are based on the position of the sun on the ecliptic. That is the annual motion track of the sun which is divided into 24 equal parts, each 15° is one part as 1° equals one day and a year equals 360°. In other words, Chinese twenty-four solar terms can be regarded as 24 seasons in China instead of the common four seasons (Spring, Summer, Fall, and Winter) which we are using internationally nowadays. The Chinese twenty-four solar terms is a fantastic refinement and summary of the change and transition of climate. It tells farmers when to sow, when to grow, and when to harvest. Surprisingly, the climate always shows significant fluctuation (e.g., obvious rise or fall of temperature, snowing, heavy rain, etc.) on the same day when a solar term falls. This kind of change of climate usually lasts in the rest of the days this solar term dominates until the next solar term arrives. Therefore, temperature and climate change stage by stage according to the arrival of each solar term, which composes the annual season (Qian, Yan and Fu, 2012). In addition, the 15-day duration of each solar term is further divided into three 5-day periods as three "Hou" in Chinese. The climate and temperature are thought to be changed more specifically at a 5-day period. Note that solar terms are not equal; 8 out of them are strong and important. Solar terms 3, 9, 15, and 21 are the beginning of each season. Each season reaches its peak at solar terms 6, 12, 18, and 24.

Nowadays, twenty-four solar terms are mostly used in traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) and its relevant treatments such as acupuncture. Solar terms have a non-negligible influence on the human body, health, and mood. Therefore, it is reasonable to say that solar terms may have an indirect impact on the stock market through affecting investors' mood at particular dates. Finding out the potential influence on the stock market would definitely be a big step forward for the value of solar terms.
