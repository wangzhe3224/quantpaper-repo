## Abstract

Cross-firm momentum effects via shared analyst coverage are well-documented in developed markets, but their robustness remains unclear in emerging markets, where information diffusion is asymmetric and analyst coverage is highly concentrated. Our work revisits this effect in an environment of extreme informational frictions — the Chinese market. We reconstruct the information transmission channel within the analyst coverage network by introducing a novel weighting scheme based on strength centrality (SC). This measure identifies influential leader firms that command disproportionate attention from both analysts and the market. Our results demonstrate that SC-weighted connected-firm returns robustly predict cross-sectional stock returns, yielding significant and persistent profits even under a rigorous stock filter. This performance cannot be subsumed by strategies based on alternative weighting schemes or by explanations such as intra-industry cross-firm momentum and information discreteness. Further analysis reveals that the superiority of the SC-based approach stems from its ability to effectively identify firms with stronger cross-period fundamental linkages. In addition, high-SC stocks are characterized by higher investor attention, more efficient information processing, lower arbitrage costs, and greater international exposures. With this evidence, we further confirm a directional spillover: cross-firm momentum effects flow exclusively from these high-SC leaders to low-SC laggards, and there is no reverse spillover. Our findings suggest that cross-firm momentum may be systematically underestimated in many international markets due to methodological limitations rather than economic irrelevance. The SC-based framework therefore offers a portable tool for global investors and researchers operating in environments with asymmetric information.

**Keywords:** Cross-firm momentum, Analyst coverage, Strength centrality, Information diffusion, Emerging markets, International exposure

## 1 Introduction

A growing body of literature documents that asset returns exhibit cross-firm predictability when firms are linked through shared information intermediaries, particularly analysts (Ali and Hirshleifer, 2020). In developed markets, most notably the U.S., strategies based on peer returns constructed via shared analyst coverage generate significant momentum profits, suggesting that information diffuses gradually across fundamentally connected firms. However, this evidence rests on institutional conditions that are far from universal: dense analyst coverage, professional investor dominance, and relatively symmetric information environments.

In contrast, the majority of international financial markets, especially emerging economies, are characterized by sparse and highly concentrated analyst attention, retail-dominated trading, and heterogeneous ownership structures. These frictions raise a fundamental question for international finance: does cross-firm momentum via shared analyst coverage persist in such environments, or is it an artifact of data-rich, institutionally mature settings?

The answer hinges on how we model the information transmission channel. Traditional approaches weight peer returns by the number of common analysts or its variants, implicitly assuming that information flows symmetrically between connected firms.[^1] However, this assumption is severely violated in many international markets, where market attention is scarce and concentrated, investor bases are heterogeneous, and price adjustment speeds vary systematically across firms. In such environments, information propagates more likely in a directional manner, specifically from a small set of highly visible "hub" firms to a long tail of followers. Under such asymmetry, traditional weighting schemes may dilute or even obscure the true signal, particularly at standard monthly horizons.[^2] Consequently, the apparent absence of cross-firm momentum in emerging markets may reflect methodological limitations rather than economic irrelevance.

[^1]: A typical approach to studying co-coverage cross-firm momentum uses the number of common analysts or its variants as weights to construct peer average returns and then tests the ability of these returns to predict the future return of a focal firm. According to Ali and Hirshleifer (2020), the number of common analysts measures the fundamental connection between firms and also gauges the cost of information processing channels for rational investors, which corresponds to an increase in the time required for information to be reflected in market outcomes.

[^2]: In markets where these resources are highly concentrated, compounded by speculative behavior from dominant retail investors, information tends to be rapidly incorporated into the prices of relevant assets. Therefore, a simple common analyst count fails to discriminate between the information diffusion dynamics of a highly influential, well-covered firm and a less-covered firm.

To address this challenge, we propose a novel weighting scheme based on a firm's strength centrality (SC) within the analyst coverage network. Specifically, SC is calculated as the sum of the weights of all edges connected to a firm, thus providing a measure of its total connectedness (Barrat et al., 2004). By definition, a high-SC firm is not only fundamentally linked to many peers but, more importantly, occupies a pivotal position as an informational hub within the entire ecosystem of analysts and, by extension, the market. Consequently, these high-SC firms become informational leaders where information is incorporated rapidly and efficiently. By assigning greater weight to returns from these high-SC stocks, we effectively construct a portfolio that pits "fast-information-incorporating" stocks against "slow-information-incorporating" stocks. This allows us to more precisely capture the gradual diffusion of information (Hong and Stein, 1999) or more generally, the process of sluggish adjustment to information (Peng and Xiong, 2006; Hou, 2007), and in turn the resultant cross-firm return predictability.

We test this framework in an environment that represents a stringent stress test for cross-firm momentum: the Chinese stock market. China exhibits extreme versions of frictions common across emerging economies: analyst coverage is highly concentrated (Xu et al., 2013), retail investors dominate trading volume (Tan et al., 2024), and ownership structures span state-owned, domestic private, and foreign-invested enterprises. Crucially, our goal is not to explain China per se, but to leverage its institutional extremity to isolate whether traditional methods fail under realistic constraints. If the SC-based signal survives where conventional approaches falter, it provides compelling evidence for its applicability to a broader class of international markets with milder but structurally similar frictions.

We begin with a univariate portfolio analysis, where the results align well with our expectations. Under a simple filtering criterion typically employed in prior studies on the Chinese market and when considering equal-weighted returns, we find that connected-firm returns (denoted as CF Ret) based on various weighting schemes significantly predict cross-sectional stock returns. Notably, the long-short portfolio formed by sorting on SC-weighted CF Ret (denoted as CF RetSC) demonstrates superior performance, yielding an equal-weighted return of 1.042% per month (annualized at 16.8%). Furthermore, all CF Ret-sorted long-short portfolios based on different weighting schemes deliver statistically significant alphas across popular risk pricing models, including the single-index model (SIM) (Sharpe, 1963), CH4 model (Liu et al., 2019), q5 model (Hou et al., 2021), and the six-factor (FF6) model (Fama and French, 2018).

However, when employing a stricter yet more practical filtering criterion that has been frequently adopted in the related literature (Hsu et al., 2018; Liu et al., 2019; Jansen et al., 2021), we find that only the CF RetSC-sorted long-short portfolio continues to deliver statistically significant equal-weighted returns and alphas across various models. In contrast, the cross-firm momentum effects based on other popular schemes weaken, and their portfolios produce significant alphas solely under the q5 and FF6 models. When further considering value-weighted returns, the results for CF RetSC remain consistent. In contrast, those of the alternatives all become insignificant, highlighting the superior performance of CF RetSC in predicting stock returns. This evidence collectively confirms the presence of more pronounced cross-firm momentum effects because of the SC weighting scheme.

We further examine whether the CF Ret-sorted long-short portfolio (denoted as CFMOM for brevity), constructed using a specific weighting scheme, can explain the performance of its counterparts based on other weighting schemes. Specifically, we focus on whether the CF RetSC-sorted long-short portfolio, denoted as CFMOMSC, can explain or be explained by other types of CFMOM. To address this question, we perform spanning tests to evaluate the alphas of the specified CFMOM, which are derived from augmented pricing factor models that incorporate the return of another type of CFMOM. Our results show that the return predictability based on CF RetSC cannot be explained by its counterparts, as evidenced by the fact that the alphas of CFMOMSC remain significant in various augmented pricing models. In other words, the cross-firm momentum effects based on the SC weighting scheme cannot be subsumed by those based on alternative weighting schemes. Conversely, CFMOMSC can largely account for the performance of the other types of CFMOM.

To validate the ability of CF RetSC to predict stock returns under different weighting schemes while controlling for additional firm attributes, we conduct the Fama and MacBeth (1973) (FM) regressions. The results of univariate FM regressions reveal that all individual CF Ret measures exhibit superior predictability for cross-sectional stock returns, even after controlling for common firm attributes. In bivariate FM regressions combining CF RetSC with each of the other types of CF Ret, we find that CF RetSC continues to result in statistically and economically significant profits. For example, the monthly average return spreads between the decile portfolios with the highest and lowest values of CF RetSC range from 8.04% to 12.48% per annum, with t-statistics exceeding 2.7. Even when all CF Ret measures are jointly incorporated in a multivariate FM regression, the return spread between the decile portfolios with the highest and lowest values of CF RetSC remains around 8.28% per annum, which is still significant at the 5% level. By contrast, in both the bivariate and multivariate setups the other CF Ret measures lose their ability to predict stock returns, either in terms of statistical or economic significance.

It is important to understand the drivers behind these findings. This essentially amounts to exploring the information embedded in SC. First, we examine the fundamental linkages underlying various weighting schemes. Using panel regressions of firms' profitability growth on the average profitability growth of their directly-connected peers, we confirm that all weighting schemes in study capture contemporaneous and cross-period fundamental connections. In comparison, the SC-based scheme excels in identifying cross-period linkages, which are more critical for cross-firm return spillovers. Second, we test whether SC can indeed identify firms that receive extensive attention from analysts and the market. Building on the results of univariate portfolio analysis linking SC to firm-level characteristics, we find that high-SC stocks associate with larger size, higher online search volume, greater analyst coverage, higher abnormal trading volume, among other attention metrics. Moreover, high-SC stocks have a significantly higher institutional ownership proportion and lower arbitrage costs. These together ensure that relevant information about high-SC stocks is incorporated into prices more quickly.

We also delve into the relationship between SC and a firm's ownership structure. This represents a key institutional dimension of the Chinese market with direct implications for a firm's information environment and, consequently, its position within the analyst network. We find that the prevalence of foreign-invested enterprises (FIEs) or firms with high international exposure decreases monotonically from the highest to the lowest SC deciles. In contrast, the proportion of state-owned enterprises (SOEs) increases monotonically as SC declines, while domestic private enterprises (DPEs) exhibit a non-linear, inverted U-shaped relationship with SC. A further scrutiny of the results reveals that although DPEs remain the largest category in the high-SC group, firms with high international exposure tend to concentrate in top network tiers, and centrally connected firms are less likely to be SOEs.

It is reasonable to expect more pronounced cross-firm momentum effects for firm laggards, which can also be identified via the SC measure. We thus conduct a bivariate portfolio analysis to evaluate the performance of portfolios formed through dependent sorting on SC and CF RetSC. Our full-sample results indicate that return predictability based on CF RetSC emerges only in the lower SC quantiles, corresponding to those stock laggards. Furthermore, as for alternative weighting schemes, we also observe that the cross-firm momentum effects are more prominent for laggards identified via SC.

It pays to further explore how peer information transmits among firms with different ownership structures. Specifically, we aim to study which type of high-SC stocks transmits peer information and which type of low-SC stocks receives this information, ultimately earning a risk premium for delayed adjustment to information. To address these concerns, we perform a bivariate portfolio analysis on sub-samples partitioned by ownership type: SOEs, DPEs, and FIEs. For each focal firm in a group, we compute three variants of the cross-firm momentum signal (CF RetSC) using peer firms from each ownership category. Within each subsample, we similarly conduct a bivariate dependent sorting.

Our analysis reveals heterogeneous roles across ownership types in cross-firm information diffusion. Low-SC DPEs act as primary recipients of delayed information, with CF RetSC from all peer types significantly predicting their returns. In contrast, low-SC FIEs show no return predictability from any peer group, confirming their role as information leaders due to faster price incorporation and higher transparency. Overall, the cross-firm momentum effect is primarily driven by slow information diffusion to peripheral DPEs, with FIEs exhibiting the faster information assimilation.

In our further tests, we consider several alternative explanations for our SC-based cross-firm momentum via shared analyst coverage. Specifically, our results suggest that our SC weighting scheme captures a unique information transmission channel, one that differs significantly from the intra-industry cross-firm momentum effects (Hou, 2007). We also find that the return predictability of CF RetSC cannot be explained by the theory of information discreteness proposed in (Huang et al., 2022). Moreover, although the CF Ret measures using weighting schemes based on different attention metrics generate significant cross-firm momentum effects, the associated return predictability can be fully subsumed by CF RetSC. In addition, we finally propose a "refined" CF Ret based on PageRank (PR) centrality (CF RetPR), which is expected to capture more comprehensive information regarding market attention. Our results show that CF RetPR exhibits superior return predictability, and this predictability cannot be explained by traditional weighting schemes, thus further validating the logic underlying our construction of the SC weighting scheme.

In summary, our findings carry direct implications for international finance. They suggest that cross-firm momentum would be systematically underestimated in many emerging markets. This pattern arises not because economic linkages are absent, but because standard methodologies fail to account for asymmetric information diffusion. Our SC-based framework thus offers a portable, network-informed tool for international investors to identify early movers and delayed adjusters in real time, even in data-scarce environments. Moreover, the finding that internationally integrated firms (e.g., foreign-invested enterprises) consistently occupy leader positions underscores a key channel through which financial openness enhances price efficiency. This mechanism is likely relevant for other emerging markets undergoing gradual financial liberalization, such as India, Vietnam, or Brazil. For policymakers in emerging markets, this implies that promoting international linkages (through foreign ownership, cross-listing, or capital account liberalization) may accelerate information diffusion and reduce mispricing. For global asset allocators, it suggests that network centrality can serve as a leading indicator of a firm's role in the international information transmission chain.

### 1.1 Related literature

A growing body of literature has explored cross-firm or cross-asset return spillovers, with two primary strands of research addressing this phenomenon. The first strand adopts data-driven or model-driven approaches.[^3] The second focuses on return spillovers driven by specific, economically-meaningful connections between firms. These connections, which serve as conduits for spillovers, can arise from shared industry sectors (Moskowitz and Grinblatt, 1999), supplier-customer relationships (Cohen and Frazzini, 2008; Menzly and Ozbas, 2010), technology adoption (Lee et al., 2019), shared analyst coverage (Ali and Hirshleifer, 2020), common geographical location (Parsons et al., 2020), production complementarity (Lee et al., 2024), and bond links (Feng et al., 2025). Specifically, in the context of the Chinese market, a number of studies have examined most of the above traditional cross-firm momentum effects (Duan et al., 2022; Yi and Guo, 2022; Jiang et al., 2023; Xiong et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2024). And more studies explore cross-firm momentum effects based on novel interconnected relationships in this market. These relationships include common investment concepts (Du et al., 2022), adjacent locations on the screen of trading software (Chen et al., 2023), and news co-mentions (Ge et al., 2025a).

[^3]: The related methods include Diebold and Yilmaz (2014) framework and its variants (Diebold et al., 2017; Ando et al., 2022), partial correlation (Kenett et al., 2015), lagged correlation (Curme et al., 2015), Granger causality (Billio et al., 2012; Zhang and Broadstock, 2020), Copula methods (Philippas and Siriopoulos, 2013; Wang et al., 2021), among others.

Our work adds to the literature on the informativeness of shared analyst coverage. Defining peer firms based on shared analyst coverage provides a useful supplement to traditional firm classifications, such as those based on industry (Kaustia and Rantala, 2021). In recent work, Gomes et al. (2023) examine the peer effects of various financial policies among firms, considering shared analyst coverage. Ali and Hirshleifer (2020) confirm the fundamental associations between firms that arise from common analysts, documenting that asset returns spread among peer firms identified by shared analysts, offering an alternative explanation for return spillovers among connected firms. Our work makes a specific contribution by providing updated evidence on the diffusion of return information based on shared analysts. Compared with prior studies, particularly those focused on the Chinese stock markets (Yi and Guo, 2022; Jiang et al., 2023; Xiong et al., 2023), which observe cross-firm momentum effects under a less stringent stock filter, we propose a refined version using centrality-based weighting schemes. Specifically, our cross-firm momentum effects remain robust and significant under a more rigorous and practical filtering criterion that has not been used in prior related studies.[^4]

[^4]: We note that Yi and Guo (2022) conduct the robustness check using different single-condition stock filters, aiming to support the existence of analyst-coverage-based cross-firm momentum in China. However, these filters do not function in the same way as the multi-condition filter typically used in most of the literature, and they cannot simultaneously avoid the impacts of stocks with "unsatisfactory" characteristics.

Furthermore, our study contributes to a small but growing literature that explicitly distinguishes between leader and laggard firms in cross-predictability. Among these, an early study by Hou (2007) adopts an industry-level perspective and demonstrates that information primarily flows from large industry leaders to smaller firms within the same industry. Scherbina and Schlusche (2020) employ a statistical methodology to define leaders as stocks that predict the returns of other stocks without being predicted by those other stocks themselves. Their work highlights the role of slow information diffusion across assets. Most recently, Ge et al. (2025b) provide a comprehensive analysis of momentum spillovers in China. They decompose news co-mention networks into directed leader-follower (LF) links and undirected peer (PE) links. Their findings indicate that the clearer, more directional LF structure facilitates faster attention spillover and price discovery. Our approach shares the goal of identifying directional spillovers but differs fundamentally from these predecessors. We move beyond the statistical identification of Scherbina and Schlusche (2020), the industry-level analysis of Hou (2007), and the news-based LF/PE decomposition of Ge et al. (2025b). Instead, we utilize the economic structure of the analyst coverage network, specifically employing the strength centrality of firms within this network. This framework allows us to define leaders ex ante based on their positional importance within a meaningful information diffusion channel. Our method provides an economically-grounded mechanism for the leadership effects documented in prior studies, which in turn offers a novel perspective and deeper insight into the cross-predictability literature.

Third, our work relates to the literature that applies network centralities to financial markets. At the micro level, existing research has focused on the role of firms or assets within investment portfolios, typically measured by centrality metrics derived from networks built upon return performance correlations (Ozsoylev and Walden, 2011; Clemente et al., 2021). At the macro level, Ozsoylev et al. (2014) use eigenvector centrality to study the relationship between an investor's position in the information network and their trading performance. El-Khatib et al. (2015) employ CEO network centrality measures to investigate how personal connections affect merger and acquisition outcomes. Rossi et al. (2018) show a positive relationship between network centrality and risk-adjusted performance in delegated investment management. Richmond (2019) explores trade network centrality to explain variation in interest rates and currency risk premia across countries, linking a nation's position in the global trade network to its economic outcomes. Evgeniou et al. (2022) investigate firm centrality in the supplier-customer network and its relationship to managerial market timing ability. In the two studies by Ali and Hirshleifer (2020) and Yi and Guo (2022) most related to our work, the complexity of firm linkages is measured by the degree and eigenvector centralities of firms within analyst coverage networks. Our study contributes to this literature by linking the importance of firms, identified through strength and PageRank centralities within an analyst coverage network, to their roles as transmitters of return information.

Section 2 outlines the construction of the analyst coverage network and the weighting schemes employed to investigate connected-firm momentum effects. Section 3 presents our baseline results. Section 4 explores the information embedded in SC. Section 5 examines the results when firm laggards are identified. Section 6 conducts further tests. Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Network Construction

### 2.1 Data

For our analysis, we use daily and monthly data for all A-share stocks listed on Chinese stock exchanges, obtained from the China Stock Market & Accounting Research (CSMAR) database. Corresponding quarterly and annual financial statement data are also sourced from the CSMAR.

Our analysis primarily focuses on firm-level characteristics constructed on a monthly basis. To ensure the robustness and reliability of our findings, we adopt a strict yet practical filter (referred to as the "rigorous filter"), which is consistent with prior literature (Hsu et al., 2018; Liu et al., 2019; Jansen et al., 2021). Specifically, stocks are excluded if they meet any of the following criteria: (1) Listed for less than six months. (2) Suspended from trading for more than five days in the past month. (3) Suspended from trading for more than 120 days in the past year. (4) Suspended on portfolio construction or rebalancing days. (5) Classified as "special treatment" (ST) or "particular transfer" (PT). (6) Locked at the daily price limit throughout the rebalancing day.[^5] (7) Among the bottom 30% in terms of market size.

[^5]: The Chinese A-share market employs a price limit system to curb excessive volatility and speculative trading. Implemented in December 1996, this system restricts daily price fluctuations to ±10% for most stocks on the Shanghai and Shenzhen main boards, with wider limits of ±20% for the ChiNext and STAR Market, and ±30% for the Beijing Exchange. Filtering stocks based on this criterion ensures our portfolios reflect realistic investment practices.

Some of the existing studies rely on relatively simple filters, usually based on a single condition, which may compromise the robustness of their findings. In our analysis, we also compare results derived from the simple filter used in their studies (also referred to as the "lax filter"), which excludes only ST and PT stocks, with those obtained using our rigorous filter that incorporates all seven conditions.[^6]

[^6]: For example, Jiang et al. (2023) and Xiong et al. (2023) exclude only ST stocks, i.e., condition (5), while Yi and Guo (2022) apply a simpler criterion, removing stocks with closing prices below five Chinese Yuan — akin to condition (7), which filters out small-size stocks. For convenience, we report baseline results using the lax filter (condition (5) alone) but also evaluate results based on each individual condition. Findings are consistent across these comparisons and are available upon request.

### 2.2 Analyst coverage network

We now introduce stock networks formed based on shared analyst coverage, referred to as the analyst coverage network. Specifically, at the beginning of each month, a firm or stock is considered covered by an analyst if the analyst has issued at least one earnings forecast or recommendation for that stock within the past 12 months. Two stocks are defined as connected if at least one analyst covers both. Using this definition, we construct monthly network structures (Ali and Hirshleifer, 2020; Gomes et al., 2023), where nodes represent firms or stocks, and edges indicate shared analyst coverage. For a focal firm $i$, its peer or connected firms are all those directly linked to $i$. From the perspective of network theory, these peers are nodes with a distance of one from the focal firm in the network structure.

**Figure 1** illustrates an example of an analyst coverage network. The figure focuses on 44 banking firms as of December 2023. Other firms are linked to these banks if they are included in at least one analyst's portfolio. It can be observed that the network based on shared analysts encapsulates information beyond the standard definition of industry sectors, as evidenced by the diverse industries of the connected firms represented by different node colors. Notably, these 44 banks are connected to firms across 34 distinct industries.[^7] Despite these interconnections, firms within the same industry, specifically within the banking sector (industry code J66), tend to exhibit closer intraconnections, as depicted in the figure.

[^7]: In our work, we adopt the 2012 version of the listed company classification method released by the China Securities Regulatory Commission (CSRC).

### 2.3 Weighting schemes and connected-firm returns

To investigate cross-firm momentum effects, we construct CFMOM portfolios by following Ali and Hirshleifer (2020). These portfolios aim to capture whether firms with connected peers exhibiting higher returns also achieve superior performance in the subsequent period. The key input for constructing these portfolios is the connected-firm return (CF Ret), defined as the weighted average return of a focal firm's connected peers in the analyst coverage network:

$$CF\ Ret^{\text{type}}_{i,t} = \sum_{j \in N_{i,t}} weight^{\text{type}}_{i,j,t} \times Ret_{j,t} \tag{1}$$

where $Ret_{j,t}$ refers to the return of stock $j$ in month $t$, adjusted for splits and dividends, and $weight_{i,j,t}$ represents the weight assigned to firm $j$. The weights are determined at the beginning of each month using data from a rolling window spanning from month $t - 1$ to month $t - 12$. The superscript type indicates different weighting schemes, which result in varying CFMOM outcomes. The set $N_{i,t}$ denotes all connected firms for the focal firm $i$.

We next review several weighting schemes employed in the literature, starting with the approach introduced by Ali and Hirshleifer (2020) (AH), which is a pioneering work on cross-firm momentum effects driven by shared analyst coverage in the U.S. stock market. Their method emphasizes the number of common analysts between stock pairs, defined as:

$$\omega^{\text{AH}}_{i,j} = n_{i,j} \tag{2}$$

where $n_{i,j}$ represents the number of analysts covering both stocks $i$ and $j$. If stocks $i$ and $j$ are not connected in the network, $n_{i,j}$ equals zero. The associated weight is scaled such that their summation is equal to one:

$$weight^{\text{AH}}_{i,j} = \frac{\omega^{\text{AH}}_{i,j}}{\sum_{j \in N_i} \omega^{\text{AH}}_{i,j}} = \frac{n_{i,j}}{\sum_{j \in N_i} n_{i,j}} \tag{3}$$

However, the AH weight fails to take into account analyst attentiveness at the individual level. Several alternative approaches can help address this issue. The first alternative comes from Israelsen (2016) (Isr), and the authors argue that forecasting errors of individual analysts are correlated across different stocks or firms. Therefore, they consider the following proxy for the correlation in consensus forecast errors between a pair of stocks:

$$\omega^{\text{Isr}}_{i,j} = \frac{n_{i,j}}{\sqrt{n_i \times n_j}} \tag{4}$$

where $n_i$ denotes the number of analysts covering firm $i$. In a similar way, we then construct $weight^{\text{Isr}}_{i,j}$ as follows:

$$weight^{\text{Isr}}_{i,j} = \frac{\omega^{\text{Isr}}_{i,j}}{\sum_{j \in N_i} \omega^{\text{Isr}}_{i,j}} = \frac{n_{i,j}/\sqrt{n_i \times n_j}}{\sum_{j \in N_i} n_{i,j}/\sqrt{n_i \times n_j}} \tag{5}$$

We notice that Sorensen (1948) (Sor) takes into account a similar measure:

$$\omega^{\text{Sor}}_{i,j} = 2 \times \frac{n_{i,j}}{n_i + n_j} \tag{6}$$

and its weight can be similarly constructed:

$$weight^{\text{Sor}}_{i,j} = \frac{\omega^{\text{Sor}}_{i,j}}{\sum_{j \in N_i} \omega^{\text{Sor}}_{i,j}} = \frac{n_{i,j}/(n_i+n_j)}{\sum_{j \in N_i} n_{i,j}/(n_i+n_j)} \tag{7}$$

Note that $n_i + n_j$ on the right-hand side of Eq. (7) counts pair analysts twice. To avoid double-counting pair analysts, a more precise measure is proposed by Muslu et al. (2014) (MRX). Specifically:

$$\omega^{\text{MRX}}_{i,j} = \frac{n_{i,j}}{n_i + n_j - n_{i,j}} \tag{8}$$

and we therefore construct $weight^{\text{MRX}}_{i,j}$ as follows:

$$weight^{\text{MRX}}_{i,j} = \frac{\omega^{\text{MRX}}_{i,j}}{\sum_{j \in N_i} \omega^{\text{MRX}}_{i,j}} = \frac{n_{i,j}/(n_i+n_j-n_{i,j})}{\sum_{j \in N_i} n_{i,j}/(n_i+n_j-n_{i,j})} \tag{9}$$

Evidently, all of the four weighting schemes mentioned above revolve around the number of common analysts for the given stock pair. According to Eq. (1), a greater number of common analysts between the focal firm $i$ and its connected firm $j$ leads to a higher weight being assigned to firm $j$. The realized return of firm $j$ thus has a stronger impact. This is also in line with the intuition that more common analysts are associated with a closer fundamental connection between firms (Ali and Hirshleifer, 2020; Jiang et al., 2023), which are expected to result in more prominent cross-firm momentum effects.

In comparison, the latter three schemes, namely $weight^{\text{Isr}}$, $weight^{\text{MRX}}$, and $weight^{\text{Sor}}$, are variations derived by scaling $weight^{\text{AH}}$ with functions involving $n_i$ and $n_j$. This approach allows for the further consideration of the impact of individual analysts covering either firm $i$ or firm $j$.[^8] Obviously, a small (large) number of individual (pair) analysts leads to a high weight being assigned to firm $j$. In this regard, these three types of weights further underscore the importance of common analysts. On the other hand, a potential risk embedded in these three weighting schemes is that they might diminish the influence of connected firms that have a large number of common analysts with respect to the focal firm. This is because in the equations, including Eq. (4), Eq. (6), and Eq. (8), the denominator changes more rapidly than the numerator, although they are positively correlated. Overall, the number of common analysts measures the fundamental connection between firms and also gauges the cost of information processing channels for rational investors (Ali and Hirshleifer, 2020), which corresponds to an increase in the time required for information to be reflected in market outcomes. Thus, classic weighting schemes that depend on the number of common analysts identify cross-firm momentum effects in mature markets such as the U.S.

[^8]: Here, we follow the convention in Muslu et al. (2014) to define individual and pair analysts. Specifically, individual analysts are those who cover either the focal firm $i$ or its connected firm $j$, but not both. Pair analysts are those who cover both the focal firm $i$ and its connected firm $j$.

However, these schemes may be suboptimal for capturing the full complexity of information diffusion in markets where analyst attention and investor processing of information are highly concentrated, and especially where retail investors dominate. This is because the classic approach implicitly assumes a relatively uniform distribution of analyst attention and information processing speed across firms. In markets where these resources are highly concentrated, compounded by speculative behavior from dominant retail investors, information tends to be rapidly incorporated into the prices of relevant assets. Therefore, a simple common analyst count fails to discriminate between the information diffusion dynamics of a highly influential, well-covered leader firm and a less-covered follower firm. The return of the leader firm, by virtue of its centrality in the information network, carries a stronger predictive signal for its peers, yet the classic scheme assigns it the same weight as the return of a peripheral follower if their pairwise common analyst count is identical. This equal weighting of fundamentally unequal information sources dilutes the momentum signal and renders the strategy suboptimal.

To successfully detect cross-firm momentum effects via shared analyst coverage, it is thus crucial to identify the firm leaders within the network, which are defined as stocks that assimilate information into their prices more swiftly. These leaders, characterized by strong fundamental ties to focal firms and robust past performance, act as the primary drivers of cross-firm momentum effects. Identifying these leaders requires assessing both the fundamental linkages between stocks and the speed at which connected firms incorporate information into their prices. The former is captured by the value of $n_{i,j}$, while the latter can be approximated by a firm's importance within the network. Specifically, a firm's importance increases with its connections to others beyond the focal firm. The broader a firm's network, the greater the attention it garners, enabling more timely incorporation of information into its stock price. As a result, these stock leaders should be assigned higher weights when evaluating their influence on the focal firm.

Fortunately, the identification of leaders or prominent nodes within a network structure is relatively straightforward, as network theory provides various well-established methods for this purpose. In particular, we adopt strength centrality (SC) (Barrat et al., 2004), aligning well with our analysis above. SC is defined as the sum of the weights of edges connected to a given node, or equivalently:

$$SC_j = \sum_{m \in N_j} n_{j,m} \tag{10}$$

We therefore readily build our SC-based weights:

$$weight^{\text{SC}}_{i,j} = \frac{SC_j}{\sum_{j \in N_i} SC_j} \tag{11}$$

By definition, $SC_j$ not only captures firm $j$'s relationship with the focal firm $i$ but also accounts for $j$'s connections with all other neighboring firms. Compared to $n_j$, $SC_j$ offers a distinct advantage in accurately reflecting the attentiveness of market participants, particularly financial analysts.[^9] The SC measure aligns powerfully with the behavioral theory of limited investor attention (Kahneman, 1973; Hirshleifer and Teoh, 2003). Moreover, investors, facing information overload, disproportionately focus on "salient" firms. The high-SC firms that naturally attract this scarce attention, also tend to be large, liquid, held by institutional investors, and associated with lower arbitrage costs.[^10] The core innovation lies in using the SC of a peer firm to weight its return when constructing the momentum signal for a focal firm. By doing this, we effectively construct a portfolio that pits "fast-information-incorporating" stocks against "slow-information-incorporating" stocks. As such, the SC scheme can more precisely capture the process of sluggish adjustment to information (Peng and Xiong, 2006; Hou, 2007). The theoretically expected significant cross-firm momentum effect can thus be interpreted as a risk premium compensating for the delayed incorporation of information from these central nodes (Hou and Moskowitz, 2005).

[^9]: It is reasonable to assume that more skilled analysts possess greater endowments of effort, including time and energy, enabling them to efficiently manage their tasks (Harford et al., 2019). Consequently, such analysts are able to cover a larger number of firms in their portfolios. Therefore, the total effort of each analyst can be approximated by the number of firms they cover. From this perspective, $SC_j$ provides a more accurate measure of the cumulative analyst effort allocated to firm $j$, whereas $n_j$ overlooks this intuitive differentiation and assumes all analysts are identical.

[^10]: Large firms naturally attract more attention due to their size and visibility (Merton, 1987; Bhushan, 1989); institutional investors, driven by research and asset allocation needs, tend to hold significant positions in these firms (O'Brien and Bhushan, 1990); and precisely because these firms have large market capitalizations, high liquidity, and high institutional ownership, the arbitrage costs—including transaction costs and short-selling costs—associated with trading their shares are significantly lower (Amihud and Mendelson, 1986; D'avolio, 2002), allowing arbitrageurs to more effectively correct any mispricing (Shleifer and Vishny, 1997) and ultimately leading to price behaviors that differ sharply from those of less visible, hard-to-arbitrage small firms (Fang and Peress, 2009).

It is also important to note that in Eq. (10), the subscript of the left-hand-side variable is only $j$, which distinguishes it from the previous four weighting schemes (see Eq. (2), Eq. (4), Eq. (6), and Eq. (8)) for firm pairs, where both $i$ and $j$ appear. This ensures that the importance of a firm, as measured by SC, remains relatively constant for different focal firms. For instance, if firm A has a higher SC value than firm B, then for each focal firm connected to both A and B, the return weight of firm A will always be higher than that of firm B. This firm importance invariance matters in detection of cross-firm momentum effects.

We now have five distinct weighting schemes. According to Eq. (1), we can therefore obtain $CF\ Ret^{\text{type}}$, where $\text{type} \in \{\text{AH, Isr, MRX, Sor, SC}\}$. Notably, the correlations among the four conventional CF Ret measures are relatively high (average Pearson correlation of 0.922). In contrast, relatively low correlations are observed between $CF\ Ret^{\text{SC}}$ and other conventional alternatives. For example, $CF\ Ret^{\text{SC}}$ has the highest Pearson correlation of 0.877 with $CF\ Ret^{\text{AH}}$, while its correlations with the other three measures drop below 0.76. Collectively, these results indicate the uniqueness of the SC-based weighting scheme.

### 2.4 Network properties

#### 2.4.1 Development of connected components

A deeper understanding of how the network structure evolves is critical to our analysis. We consider three types of network configurations that may emerge in our samples. In the first configuration, many stocks remain unconnected to any other within the network. In such cases, these focal firms have few peer firms, rendering it impossible to construct connected-firm returns using the previously outlined method. The second configuration, which is likely more prevalent, consists of several independent stock groups (or communities) within the network, with no interconnections between these groups. The third and also relatively ideal configuration includes multiple components within the network, where the majority of firms belong to the largest connected component.

We therefore present the evolution of connected components in analyst coverage networks from December 2001 to December 2023 in **Figure 2**. As shown in the figure, each of the networks for the period from 2001 to 2007 consists of numerous components. By contrast, the network structure undergoes a significant shift beginning in 2009. A single dominant component consistently emerges, encompassing the majority of firms.

As a result, our analysis based on analyst coverage network begins in January 2010. Monthly networks are constructed using 12-month rolling samples starting from January 2009, giving rise to the main results covering the period from January 2010 to December 2023. We finally obtain 363,008 month-firm observations, with an average of 2,161 stocks per month.

#### 2.4.2 Statistics of the components

We further evaluate key structural properties of the largest components in the monthly network structures from 2010 to 2023. **Table 1** presents the results.

The results in Table 1 reveal that the number of firms included in the largest component ranges from a minimum of 1,366 to a maximum of 3,103, with both the median and mean at 2,216. The proportion of these firms relative to the total number of stocks varies between 52.4% and 92.9%, averaging 72.6%. In contrast, the market capitalization of firms within the largest component consistently accounts for a significant share of the total market capitalization. For instance, the lowest observed proportion is 85%, while both the mean and median are 91%, peaking at 96.8%.

We also calculate the percentage of cross-industry connections for each firm and the industry distribution of the associated stocks. Our results show that approximately 70% of connections occur between stocks from different industries. For a focal firm, it connects directly to stocks from an average of 24 industries, with the maximum number of connected industries reaching 63. These findings align with the patterns presented in Figure 1, which demonstrate that firm connections based on analyst coverage differ significantly from those based on the traditional industry definition (Kaustia and Rantala, 2021).

By examining the maximum edge weight that represents the most frequently co-covered stock pairs, we find that at least 38 analysts simultaneously cover these pairs, with a maximum of 83 analysts and an average of 60 analysts. Despite this, the network density of the largest component remains relatively low, ranging from 4.9% to 14.3%, with an average of 7.7%. These findings suggest that the connectivity within the largest component resembles a scale-free network (Albert and Barabási, 2002): most stock pairs are covered by relatively few analysts, reflecting a structure shaped by market segmentation and analyst specialization, which likely impacts market efficiency and investment strategies.

### 3.1 Cross-firm momentum effects under different weighting schemes

We begin by examining cross-firm momentum effects using the aforementioned weighting schemes. At the beginning of each month, individual stocks are sorted into ten decile groups based on their latest connected-firm returns, i.e., $CF\ Ret^{\text{AH}}$, $CF\ Ret^{\text{Isr}}$, $CF\ Ret^{\text{Sor}}$, $CF\ Ret^{\text{MRX}}$, and $CF\ Ret^{\text{SC}}$. This corresponds to a monthly average return spread between the decile portfolios with the highest and lowest values of $CF\ Ret^{\text{SC}}$ of 1.34%, resulting in an annual return of 16.08%.

By contrast, the slope coefficients of $CF\ Ret^{\text{SC}}$ in all four models remain statistically significant at the 5% level, with t-statistics exceeding 2.7. The monthly average return spreads between the highest and lowest decile portfolios of $CF\ Ret^{\text{SC}}$ range from approximately 0.67% to 1.04%, corresponding to annual returns of 8.04% to 12.48%.

Model (10) includes all five CF Ret versions. The slope for $CF\ Ret^{\text{SC}}$ remains statistically significant at the 5% level, evidenced by a t-statistic of 2.41. The return spread between the decile portfolios is around 0.69% per month, equivalent to 8.28% per annum. Meanwhile, the coefficients for other CF Ret versions are insignificant at the 5% level. Those for $CF\ Ret^{\text{AH}}$, $CF\ Ret^{\text{Isr}}$, and $CF\ Ret^{\text{MRX}}$ even turn negative. These results together suggest the superior ability of return forecasting based on $CF\ Ret^{\text{SC}}$.

**Panel B of Table 5** presents results using weighted least squares (WLS) methodology from Asparouhova et al. (2013). Overall, we observe similar or even better performance for $CF\ Ret^{\text{SC}}$. The slope coefficient increases to 0.123, with a comparable t-statistic of 3.96. In the multivariate FM regressions, the slopes and t-statistics of $CF\ Ret^{\text{SC}}$ show slight increases relative to those in Panel A, while the coefficients for the other four CF Ret measures become insignificant at the 5% level.

## 4 The Information Embedded in the SC Weighting Scheme

### 4.1 Fundamental linkages

We first examine whether fundamental linkages exist for stock pairs under various weighting schemes. We hypothesize that these linkages play a significant role in cross-firm momentum effects, with stronger linkages expected to result in more pronounced return spillovers.

To test this hypothesis, we regress firms' profitability growth measures on the average growth measures of their connected firms.[^12] The measure of profitability growth (PG) for each stock is defined as:

$$PG_{i,t} = 2 \times \frac{Profit_t - Profit_{t-1}}{Assets_t + Assets_{t-1}} \tag{12}$$

where $Profit$ and $Assets$ denote the operating income before depreciation and total assets, respectively. Similarly, we can construct the average PG performance for connected firms, denoted as CF PG:

$$CF\ PG^{\text{type}}_{i,t} = \sum_{j \in N_{i,t}} weight^{\text{type}}_{i,j,t} \times PG_{j,t}, \quad \text{type} = \{\text{AH, Isr, Sor, MRX, SC}\} \tag{13}$$

[^12]: We also consider another widely-used variable related to firm fundamentals, namely sales growth, which is defined as $(sales\_per\_share_t/sales\_per\_share_{t-1}) - 1$. However, our unreported results indicate no evidence of fundamental linkages between focal firms and their peers under any of weighting schemes in our study, suggesting that sales growth is unsuitable for our analysis.

We build the following panel regression models to evaluate the relationship between $CF\ PG_t$ and $PG_t$, as well as between $CF\ PG_t$ and $PG_{t+1}$:

$$PG_{i,t} = \beta_1 \times CF\ PG^{\text{type}}_{i,t} + Controls + YearEffect + FirmEffect + \epsilon_{i,t} \tag{14}$$

$$PG_{i,t+1} = \beta_1 \times CF\ PG^{\text{type}}_{i,t} + Controls + YearEffect + FirmEffect + \epsilon_{i,t} \tag{15}$$

Both regressions include year and firm fixed effects and the logarithm of market equity in the control variables.

The results are presented in **Table 6**. As shown in models (1) to (5) in Panel A, a contemporaneously close fundamental relationship between PG and CF PG is observed across different weighting schemes, as indicated by their large t-statistics exceeding eight. In contrast, relatively weaker fundamental linkages are evident in the SC-based results. Specifically, the slope of $CF\ PG^{\text{SC}}$ is 0.052, whereas the slopes for the other four counterparts exceed 0.08.

Further evidence from models (6) to (9) reinforces this finding. After controlling individually for the four counterparts in the panel regression, the contemporaneous relationship between $CF\ PG^{\text{SC}}$ and PG becomes insignificant and even negative in some cases, whereas other versions of CF PG remain statistically significant. These findings collectively suggest that the SC-based weighting scheme is not well-suited for accurately identifying contemporaneous fundamental linkages.

Turning to the cross-period results in Panel B, we observe a different pattern. Unlike the findings in Panel A, the cross-period relationship between PG and $CF\ PG^{\text{SC}}$ emerges as the strongest among the weighting schemes. This is evidenced by a large slope coefficient of 0.048 for $CF\ PG^{\text{SC}}$, accompanied by a high t-statistic of 7.02. Furthermore, the relationship between PG and $CF\ PG^{\text{SC}}$ remains robust after controlling for other alternatives. Specifically, the slopes for $CF\ PG^{\text{SC}}$ exceed 0.036, with associated t-statistics surpassing 4.7. In contrast, the slopes for $CF\ PG^{\text{Isr}}$, $CF\ PG^{\text{MRX}}$, and $CF\ PG^{\text{Sor}}$ drop below 0.025, though they remain statistically significant at the 5% level. Additionally, the slope for $CF\ PG^{\text{AH}}$ even becomes insignificant.

In summary, these findings demonstrate that within the analyst coverage network, connected firms exhibit both contemporaneous and cross-period fundamental linkages under various weighting schemes. However, the SC-based weighting scheme proves more effective in identifying cross-period fundamental connections, which are evidently more critical for the cross-firm momentum effects identified in the previous sections.

### 4.2 Average firm attributes

Another key reason that the SC-based weighting scheme leads to more significant cross-firm momentum lies in the fact that SC captures differences in the level of investor attention. This section therefore examines the relationship between SC and relevant firm-level characteristics.

We first consider the following traditional attention metrics: the logarithm of firm size (LnSize); the number of analysts covering the firm (NOA); the search volume index with stock codes as keywords (SVIC); the search volume index with stock codes, company abbreviations, and full company names as keywords (SVIA); abnormal trading volume in Chinese Yuan (AbnVolume); cumulative returns over the past 12 months (CSMOM).[^13] We also construct an attention metric that relies on real market reactions, which describes stock performance during a firm's earnings announcement window — the absolute value of cumulative abnormal returns during the earnings announcement window (AbsCAR).

[^13]: The data on the Search Volume Index (SVI) for Chinese listed firms are derived from the Chinese Research Data Services Platform (CNRDS). SVI is a composite search index calculated using Baidu platform data.

Aside from these attention metrics, we consider proxy variables for other channels related to the incorporation of information into prices. Specifically, we use the proportion of institutional ownership (InstProp) as a proxy variable for information dissemination efficiency. We follow Gu et al. (2018) to construct a limit-to-arbitrage (LoA) index.

Beyond these conventional firm attributes, we delve into a firm's ownership structure. We examine three distinct types of ownership: (1) High international-exposure firms — QFII ownership proportion (QFIIProp) and the dummy variable of foreign-invested enterprise (DFIE). (2) State-owned enterprises (SOEs) — the dummy variable DSOE. (3) Domestic private enterprises (DPEs) — the dummy variable DDPE.

The results are shown in **Table 7**. Consistent with our prior predictions, the results confirm that high-SC stocks correspond to a higher level of market attention. As SC decreases, all attention metrics exhibit a downward trend. Specifically, high-SC (low-SC) stocks associate with larger (smaller) size, higher (lower) online search volume, greater (fewer) analyst coverage, higher (lower) abnormal trading volume, and more pronounced (less pronounced) return reactions during earnings announcement windows.

We also find that high-SC stocks have a significantly higher institutional ownership proportion (InstProp), which decreases monotonically as SC declines. A similar monotonic pattern emerges in the relationship between LoA and SC. High-SC stocks correspond to smaller LoA values, meaning lower arbitrage costs, while low-SC stocks have significantly higher arbitrage costs. These results indicate that the stock leaders captured by SC not only attract widespread attention but also involve more efficient information processing by investors and lower costs in actual trading. These together ensure that relevant information about high-SC stocks is incorporated into prices more quickly.

We next examine the relationship between SC and a firm's ownership structure. As shown in the last four rows of the table, patterns in ownership structure across SC reveal significant differences. Specifically, the prevalence of FIEs exhibits a monotonically decreasing trend from the highest to the lowest SC deciles. In stark contrast, the proportion of SOEs exhibits a monotonically increasing trend as we move from the highest-SC to the lowest-SC deciles. The proportion of DPEs displays a non-linear, inverted U-shaped relationship with SC.

For the high-SC portfolio, the proportions of FIEs, SOEs, and DPEs are 10.9%, 39.8%, and 48.5%, respectively. Relative to the average levels (7.8%, 44.2%, and 47.5%), the proportion of FIEs is "unusually" high, while that of SOEs is lower. Although DPEs still account for the highest proportion, comparisons reveal that high international-exposure firms are more likely to concentrate in the top tiers of the network, and most centrally connected firms in the network are less likely to be SOEs. Overall, the clear stratification of ownership types across the SC spectrum strongly supports our hypothesis that the analyst network is shaped by a firm's integration into global capital markets and its domestic institutional role.

## 5 Identification of Laggards

### 5.1 Full-sample analysis

Recall that our SC weighting scheme is designed to capture the channel through which information transmits from fast-information-incorporating stocks to slow-information-incorporating stocks. Given that our results above show low-SC firms attract relatively less attention, exhibit lower information processing efficiency, and face higher arbitrage costs, it is reasonable to expect more pronounced cross-firm momentum effects when laggards are identified by SC.

To test this conjecture, we perform a bivariate portfolio analysis. Specifically, we first divide stocks into five quintile groups based on their SC values. Then, within each SC quintile, we further sort stocks into five quintile groups based on $CF\ Ret^{\text{SC}}$. The results are presented in **Table 8**.

In the equal-weighted results, we observe that the $CF\ Ret^{\text{SC}}$-sorted long-short portfolios yield statistically significant returns only in SC group 3 and lower SC groups, at the 5% level. Furthermore, significant return spreads and notable alphas are observed solely in SC group 4 and the Low group. The long-short portfolio returns exhibit a strong non-linear relationship across different SC groups. Notably, the monthly average returns for the High SC group and SC group 2 are both below 0.4%, whereas the results for SC groups 3, 4, and 5 exceed a monthly average return of 0.7%. The significant returns primarily stem from improved performance in the long positions. Specifically, the returns generated by High $CF\ Ret^{\text{SC}}$ portfolios in SC groups 1 and 2 range from 0.7% to 0.83% per month. In contrast, in SC groups 3 to 5, these returns sharply increase to over 1.11%.

In the value-weighted results, our primary findings remain consistent. Specifically, we observe significant results only in SC groups 3 and 5. Notably, only in SC group 5 does the $CF\ Ret^{\text{SC}}$-sorted long-short portfolio generate a significant return spread, along with notable alphas across all pricing models considered.

While the other four weighting schemes do not assign proper weights to the focal firm's connected leaders and laggards, they still provide some information related to this distinction. Overall, our findings are consistent: in the low SC quantile groups, corresponding to laggards, the CF Ret-sorted long-short portfolios under different weighting schemes exhibit significant return performance.[^14]

[^14]: It is also worth noting that our result above does not contradict the findings in Ali and Hirshleifer (2020). In fact, these two results are not comparable. Ali and Hirshleifer (2020) employ the degree and eigenvector centralities of firms to characterize the complexity of firm linkages. We emphasize that there are significant differences between the degree centrality and strength centrality.

### 5.2 Sub-sample analysis

In the results of the previous section, we observe distinct distribution patterns of firms with different ownership structures across ten SC deciles. An intriguing question arises: how does peer information transmit among firms with different ownership structures?

To address the above concerns, we conduct a detailed bivariate portfolio analysis on partitioned subsamples. We first segment the entire universe of stocks into three distinct groups based on their ultimate ownership: SOEs, DPEs, and FIEs. For each focal firm in each ownership group, we construct three versions of the cross-firm momentum signal ($CF\ Ret^{\text{SC}}$). Each version is computed using a different set of peer firms: one consisting exclusively of SOE peers, another of DPE peers, and a third of FIE peers. This 3×3 research design yields nine independent analyses. The results are presented in **Table 9**.

Our sub-sample analysis based on ownership structure unveils the heterogeneous roles played by different types of firms in the cross-firm information diffusion process. The results indicate that low-SC DPEs act as the primary recipients of delayed information. This is evidenced by the significant predictive power of $CF\ Ret^{\text{SC}}$ constructed from all three types of peer firms (SOE, DPE, and FIE) for their future returns.

In contrast, low-SC SOEs exhibit a moderate delay, primarily in response to information originating from SOE and FIE peers, as shown by significant alphas in some models. This partial integration suggests that while SOEs are not entirely immune to information spillovers, their distinct institutional setting and policy-driven nature modulate the diffusion process.

Most importantly, we find no statistically significant monthly return predictability for low-SC FIEs using any peer group. This null result is not a failure of the measure but a crucial confirmation of their role as information leaders. It suggests that information relevant to FIEs is incorporated into their prices with relative speed, leaving little delayed reaction to be captured at the monthly frequency. This finding aligns with the view that FIEs, characterized by higher transparency and scrutiny from sophisticated global investors, reside in the most informationally efficient segment of the market.

In summary, the aggregate cross-firm momentum phenomenon is predominantly fueled by the slow information diffusion toward the peripheral DPEs. The market exhibits a multi-speed information incorporation process, where the pace is fastest for internationally exposed FIEs and slowest for lesser-known private firms, with SOEs occupying an intermediate space.

## 6 Further Tests

In this section, we further consider several alternative explanations regarding our SC-based cross-firm momentum via shared analyst coverage.[^15] We also examine the results based on an alternative network centrality. For the following tests, we rely primarily on the Fama-MacBeth (FM) framework. To facilitate interpretation, we scale the coefficients so that they represent the change in a stock's monthly average excess return associated with a one-standard-deviation increase in each right-hand-side variable.

[^15]: We thank the anonymous reviewers for motivating us to consider these issues.

### 6.1 Intra-industry cross-firm momentum

Hou (2007) proposes an industry-based channel of information diffusion. A potential concern is that our SC weighting scheme may capture a mechanism similar to the role of industry leaders, or intra-industry cross-firm momentum effects.

First, we construct industry-based peer returns ($CF\ Ret^{\text{Ind}}$). We aim to examine whether $CF\ Ret^{\text{Ind}}$ explains the return predictability of $CF\ Ret^{\text{SC}}$. In a further design, we reconstruct $CF\ Ret^{\text{SC}}$ by excluding firms in the same industry that are directly connected to the focal firm.

The results appear in the first six columns of **Table 10**. Results in column (1) show that a one-standard-deviation increase in $CF\ Ret^{\text{Ind}}$ generates a monthly average return of 0.275% (t = 3.22), indicating the presence of significant intra-industry cross-firm momentum in the market. When we include $CF\ Ret^{\text{Ind}}$ as an additional control variable, the return predictive power of $CF\ Ret^{\text{SC}}$ remains significant, with a one-standard-deviation increase leading to an average return of 0.302% per month (t = 4.29). Meanwhile, the slope of $CF\ Ret^{\text{Ind}}$ also retains statistical significance.

In column (3), we test $CF\ Ret^{\text{SC}}$ reconstructed after excluding peers within the same industry, and find that $CF\ Ret^{\text{SC}}$ retains its significant return predictive power. Even when including $CF\ Ret^{\text{Ind}}$ as an additional control, a one-standard-deviation increase in $CF\ Ret^{\text{SC}}$ still generates a monthly average return of 0.261% (t = 4.95).

In contrast, if we exclude peers based on shared analyst coverage, the performance of reconstructed $CF\ Ret^{\text{Ind}}$ deteriorates substantially. Column (5) shows that its slope declines to 0.143, a value equal to half of its original magnitude, and it no longer achieves statistical significance at the 5% level.

The findings above firmly indicate that our SC weighting scheme captures a unique information transmission channel that differs significantly from the traditional industry-based channel.

### 6.2 Information discreteness

Another alternative explanation is related to cognitive trigger of investors. In light of Huang et al. (2022), when the return information of the leading firm is continuous (unlike that associated with typical news events), investors remain inattentive, resulting in insufficient information response and a strong lag effect.

To examine how the theory of information discreteness affects the robustness of our results, we follow Da et al. (2014) and Huang et al. (2022) to define information discreteness (ID) as follows:

$$ID_{i,t} = sgn(CRET) \times [\%neg_{i,t} - \%pos_{i,t}] \tag{16}$$

where the cumulative return of $CF\ Ret^{\text{SC}}$ during the past $J$-month ($J = 3, 6, 12$) period is denoted CRET. A high ID value indicates a high degree of information discreteness, while a low ID indicates a high degree of information continuity.

As reported in **Table 11**, we do not observe more significant profits on $CF\ Ret^{\text{SC}}$-sorted long-short portfolios in the low ID group. Instead, the results are more significant in the high ID group.

To further clarify the relationship between ID and SC, we construct CF Ret based on the ID measure. Columns (7) to (9) of Table 10 present results from univariate FM regressions. Our results confirm that ID-based CF Ret measures all possess significant return predictability. Specifically, a one-standard-deviation increase in each ID-based CF Ret generates at least a 0.355% monthly average return, with adjusted t-statistics above 3.7.

In contrast, columns (10) to (12) demonstrate that adding $CF\ Ret^{\text{SC}}$ to these regressions substantially reduces the return predictability of ID-based CF Ret measures. Their associated coefficients approach zero and lose statistical significance. By comparison, $CF\ Ret^{\text{SC}}$ retains both economic and statistical significance, as its one-standard-deviation increase generates at least a significant 0.287% monthly average return. Our findings indicate that cross-firm momentum based on the SC weighting scheme cannot be explained by the theory of information discreteness.

### 6.3 The schemes based on attention metrics

According to the construction of SC, it incorporates both fundamental linkages information and measures a peer firm's systemic importance within the information intermediary network, which differs significantly from the information contained in traditional attention metrics. However, our results show that a firm's SC is indeed closely related to common attention metrics, raising a natural question: do weighting schemes based on these attention metrics perform better than their SC-based counterpart?

To address this issue, we construct their respective CF Ret measures following the approach used to build $CF\ Ret^{\text{SC}}$, which relies on the attention metrics presented in Table 7, namely Size, NOA, CSMOM, AbnVolume, AbsCAR, SVIC, and SVIA.

The results appear in **Table 12**. Clearly, all CF Ret based on attention-related metrics retain significantly positive return predictability. Specifically, a one-standard-deviation change in the coefficient generates an average monthly return of at least 0.277%, and most adjusted t-statistics exceed three.

In the second specification, we include $CF\ Ret^{\text{SC}}$ and each attention-related CF Ret simultaneously. As shown in the right panel of the table, the slope coefficients of $CF\ Ret^{\text{SC}}$ all remain significant at the 5% level. A one-standard-deviation increase generates an average monthly return of at least 0.354%, and all adjusted t-statistics exceed 2.53. At the same time, the other CF Ret measures lose their return predictability. Our results confirm that $CF\ Ret^{\text{SC}}$ can subsume the predictive power of these attention-related CF Ret measures, thus indicating the outperformance of our SC weighting scheme.

### 6.4 An alternative centrality

While the SC captures the influence of a firm's directly connected peers, it fails to fully account for broader indirect influences that extend beyond these direct relationships or the impact of peers of peers. To address this limitation, we introduce PageRank (PR) centrality to serve this purpose (Page et al., 1999). By taking into account not only the direct connections of a firm but also its peers' connections, PR captures the indirect influence exerted through the network.

We construct PR-based CF Ret, i.e., $CF\ Ret^{\text{PR}}$, and re-conduct the tests. Our results demonstrate that raw returns and alphas of $CF\ Ret^{\text{PR}}$-sorted long-short portfolio range from 0.82% to 1.35% per month, which surpass the performance of the $CF\ Ret^{\text{SC}}$-sorted long-short portfolio. Moreover, the results of FM regressions confirm that $CF\ Ret^{\text{PR}}$ has superior return forecasting ability, which cannot be explained by four classic CF Ret versions and related firm-level characteristics.

## 7 Conclusions

This study introduces a network centrality-based approach that refines the detection of cross-firm momentum by explicitly accounting for a firm's informational leadership within analyst coverage networks. We demonstrate that weighting peer returns by their strength centrality (SC), which is a measure of their overall connectedness in the network, significantly enhances the predictability of future returns. Unlike conventional common-analyst-based measures, the SC-weighted strategy produces robust and persistent returns even after controlling for common risk factors and the related firm-level characteristics. Its performance also cannot be subsumed by strategies based on alternative weighting schemes or by explanations such as intra-industry cross-firm momentum and information discreteness.

The effectiveness of SC stems from its ability to pinpoint firms that serve as informational hubs. High-SC firms attract greater attention from analysts and institutional investors, operate with lower arbitrage constraints, and incorporate information more rapidly into their stock prices. As a result, returns of these central firms lead and positively predict returns of less central firms connected through shared coverage. More importantly, we document a clear directional effect: cross-firm momentum spreads from high-SC leaders to low-SC laggards, with no evidence of reverse spillovers. This leader-laggard mechanism, crystallized by the SC measure, is likely to be a fundamental feature of markets with concentrated information flow and heterogeneous investors.

Our findings offer a novel perspective on how information travels through financial markets and provide practical insights for constructing trading strategies and managing systematic risk in markets where information asymmetry is prominent. Our findings suggest that cross-firm momentum may be hidden (not absent) in many international markets due to methodological limitations in existing approaches. The SC-based method provides a generalizable way to capture leadership in networks and improves upon existing measures by emphasizing economic significance over mere connectedness. For global investors, it offers a real-time, network-informed tool to identify early movers and delayed adjusters even in data-scarce settings. For policymakers, it underscores how financial openness and institutional design shape a firm's role in the information ecosystem.

Our future studies aim to extend this approach to other novel weighted network structures, such as those based on news co-mentions (Ge et al., 2025a) or common investment concepts (Du et al., 2022), to further explore the architecture of information diffusion in global and cross-border markets.
