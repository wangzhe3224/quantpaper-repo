## Highlight

- **Alpha idea:** A portfolio-independent estimation method for q-theory investment-based asset pricing models — model parameters are obtained via Bayesian Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) to match the *entire sample of firm-level stock returns*, rather than the average returns of a chosen set of testing portfolios, directly addressing Campbell's (2017) critique that "different parameters are needed to fit each anomaly."
- **Data sources:** CRSP (monthly stock returns); Compustat annual and quarterly industrial files (accounting data); statutory corporate income tax rates from the Commerce Clearing House annual publications.
- **Universe:** All common stocks traded on NYSE, Amex, and NASDAQ with available accounting and return data, excluding financial firms (SIC 6000–6999), firms with negative book equity, and firms with nonpositive total assets, net PP&E, or sales at portfolio formation.
- **Sample period:** January 1967 to June 2017 (fundamental portfolio returns June 1967 to December 2016); 136,598 firm-level observations; Fama–French 10-industry classification (2 × 500 latent parameters estimated for the 1967–2016 sample).
- **Signal construction:** The two-capital q-model of Gonçalves, Xue, and Zhang (2020) implies a firm-level "fundamental return" $r^F_{it+1}$ from accounting variables; two structural parameters — production curvature $\gamma$ and physical investment adjustment cost $a$ — are estimated by Bayesian MCMC under four specifications (constant, industry-specific, time-varying, and industry-and-time-varying). Anomalies are evaluated on 120 decile portfolios (10 per anomaly) sorted on 12 variables across six categories.
- **Key finding (fit):** Under the best-fitting industry-and-time-varying specification ($\theta_{jt}$), fundamental returns match the mean/skewness/kurtosis of realized returns and capture 30%–56% of return volatility; the average contemporaneous correlation between realized and fundamental returns is **0.69 for decile portfolios and 0.43 for high-minus-low portfolios** (all significant at 1%) — far exceeding prior literature's near-zero "correlation puzzle."
- **Key finding (anomalies):** The estimated model generates large and significant **size, momentum, profitability, investment, and intangibles** premiums, but **fails to generate the value and accruals premiums** with the correct sign — illustrating that a model's anomaly-explaining ability is overstated when parameters are chosen to match the very anomaly returns it tries to explain.

## Abstract

This paper proposes a portfolio-independent method to estimate q-theory models, in which parameters are obtained using Bayesian Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) to match firm-level stock returns. Our methodology addresses Campbell's (2017) critique on prior studies that model parameters are chosen to fit a specific set of anomalies and different values are needed to fit each anomaly. By targeting the entire sample of firm-level returns and allowing industry and time variations in parameter values, our estimations yield higher correlations between realized and fundamental portfolio returns compared to prior literature. Additionally, the estimated two-capital model generates large and significant size, momentum, profitability, investment, and intangibles premiums, but falls short in explaining the value and accruals anomalies. This limitation underscores the importance of portfolio-independent parameter estimation in evaluating a model's capability to generate return anomalies.

**Keywords:** q-theory, Bayesian MCMC estimation, Anomalies, Investment, Profitability

## 1. Introduction

The investment-based asset pricing literature (Cochrane, 1991; Gomes, Kogan, and Zhang, 2003; Zhang, 2005, among others) studies returns from the supply side of the economy and formulates returns based on firm fundamentals, under the assumption that a firm operates to maximize its market equity.[^1] Liu, Whited, and Zhang (2009) show that such a simple q-model fits value, earnings surprises, and investment anomalies well, when model parameters are chosen to match the average observed stock returns of decile portfolios sorted by these anomaly variables. Additional asset pricing anomalies and firm valuation ratios can be explained in this framework as shown in subsequent studies, such as Belo, Xue, and Zhang (2013), Liu and Zhang (2014), and Gonçalves, Xue, and Zhang (2020), among others.

In this line of research, model parameters are estimated via the Generalized Method of Moments (GMM) with the average observed returns of testing portfolios as target moments. This practice often generates parameter estimates that are portfolio-dependent. As Campbell (2017) (page 213) puts it: *"This problem, that different parameters are needed to fit each anomaly, is a pervasive one in the q-theoretic asset pricing literature."* The set of parameters that are chosen to explain their target anomalies often fails to explain other anomalies. Therefore, this approach can overstate the model's capability in explaining the target anomalies. A recent work by Gonçalves, Xue, and Zhang (2020) addresses Campbell's critique by showing that a two-capital q-model estimated using a precise aggregation method fits well the value, momentum, profitability, and investment anomalies with a single set of structural parameters. In this study, we go a step further and propose a portfolio-independent estimation method.

To address Campbell's (2017) critique, we propose a portfolio-independent methodology in which model parameters are chosen using the Bayesian Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) method to match the entire sample of individual firm-level stock returns. Bayesian MCMC is widely used in the field of macroeconomics (Smets and Wouters, 2007, among others) and has also been used to study return dynamics (Li, Wells, and Yu, 2008; Li, Li, Wang, and Yu, 2019, among others). Given its portfolio-independence, this methodology can be used to evaluate the capability of a given model in explaining stock market anomalies.

For comparison with prior literature, we estimate the same two-capital q-model as in Gonçalves, Xue, and Zhang (2020) and examine its performance in explaining anomalies with portfolio-independent parameters. Using the 40 value, profitability, investment, and momentum decile portfolios as test assets, Gonçalves, Xue, and Zhang (2020) show that the estimated model can successfully generate these four anomaly premiums. Different from their approach, we estimate the same model to match firm-level stock returns instead and ask whether the estimated model can generate a variety of return anomalies in data. Our simulation studies show that Bayesian MCMC is able to discover the true parameter values under this model framework and performs better than the GMM and Nonlinear Least Squares (NLS) methods. With the flexibility of Bayesian MCMC, we estimate the model under four specifications, each with constant, industry-specific, time-varying, or industry-specific and time-varying parameter values.

To examine the capability of the model in explaining stock market anomalies, we consider 12 well-documented anomalies covering six major categories classified by Hou, Xue, and Zhang (2020):[^2]

- **value** anomaly sorted on book-to-market equity ratio (BM);
- **momentum** anomaly sorted on the prior 11-month returns skipping the most recent month (R11);
- four **investment** anomalies sorted on asset growth (I/A), net stock issues (NSI), investment-to-assets ratio (∆PI/A), and accruals (Accruals);
- three **profitability** anomalies sorted on return-on-equity (ROE), return-on-assets (ROA), and gross profitability (GP/A);
- two **intangibles** anomalies sorted on R&D expenses-to-market ratio (RD/M) and advertising expenses-to-market ratio (Ad/M);
- one **trading frictions** anomaly sorted on market capitalization (Size).

Several findings emerge from our study. **First**, the estimated q-model matches the mean, skewness, and kurtosis of firm-level returns well under all four specifications and is able to capture 30% to 56% of the volatility depending on the specification. The specification with industry-specific and time-varying parameter values leads to significantly lower mean absolute error (m.a.e.) than the other three specifications. We conduct an extensive set of tests to confirm that the industry and time variations in our estimates of the two model parameters — the investment adjustment cost parameter and production curvature parameter — are largely consistent with their economic interpretations.

**Second**, the model-implied firm-level stock returns (hereafter, *fundamental returns*) under all four specifications exhibit large and significant size, momentum, investment, profitability, and intangibles premiums.[^3] More importantly, the estimated models capture the dynamics of the 120 decile portfolios (10 for each anomaly) very well. Specifically, under the specification with industry-specific and time-varying parameter values, the returns of the fundamental and realized decile portfolio are all highly correlated (correlation coefficients are all significant at the 1% level). The average correlation coefficient is **0.69** for the decile portfolios and **0.43** for the high-minus-low portfolios. The comparison among the four specifications indicates that both the industry and time variations in parameter values are critical to the high correlations for the high-minus-low portfolios, while only time variation is critical to the high correlations for the decile portfolios.

These high correlations contrast sharply with prior studies, where the average anomaly premiums are well-matched but the correlations between fundamental and realized portfolio returns are generally close to zero, or even negative. This phenomenon is termed as "the correlation puzzle" by Liu, Whited, and Zhang (2009). Utilizing an advanced aggregation method, Gonçalves, Xue, and Zhang (2020) are able to generate mostly positive correlations. However, among the 44 BM, R11, I/A, and ROE decile and high-minus-low portfolios studied by Gonçalves, Xue, and Zhang (2020), only 10 out of 44 correlations are positive and significant (at least at the 10% level) as shown in Panel B of their Table 6, compared to 21 out of 44 under our specification with constant parameters. This comparison indicates that matching firm-level stock returns, rather than the average returns of test portfolios, also helps to generate higher correlations.

**Last but not least**, none of the four specifications generates significant value premium and accruals premium with the correct sign. The small estimated value of the investment adjustment costs parameter is the reason why our estimations fail to generate the value premium, whereas the one in Gonçalves, Xue, and Zhang (2020) successfully does. We use the same model and data sample as theirs, with the only difference being that their estimation targets the book-to-market decile portfolio returns, while we target firm-level returns. This result illustrates that a model's ability to explain anomalies is likely overstated if its parameters are selected to match the anomaly returns it aims to explain. Depending on methods and datasets, estimates of the investment adjustment costs parameter in the literature cover a wide range. Our estimates are in similar magnitude as those in the influential work of Cooper and Haltiwanger (2006), among others. Note that our model's failure to generate the value premium should not be interpreted as a failure of the investment-based asset pricing models because our model is highly stylized. Models in the asset pricing literature that generate the value premium often feature asymmetric investment adjustment costs, which is absent in our model.[^4]

Our work is built directly on Liu, Whited, and Zhang (2009), Liu and Zhang (2014), and Gonçalves, Xue, and Zhang (2020). These papers conduct GMM estimations of various q-models using average anomaly portfolio returns as target moments. Liu, Whited, and Zhang (2009) show that a one-capital q-model can match the average returns of portfolios sorted on earnings surprises, book-to-market equity, and capital investment. Liu and Zhang (2014) use the same model and estimation procedure to explain the momentum premium. However, the estimated parameter values vary with testing portfolios substantially. Gonçalves, Xue, and Zhang (2020) estimate a two-capital q-model to match the average returns of 40 decile portfolios sorted on book-to-market equity, asset growth, return-on-equity, and momentum. They show that when fundamental returns are computed at firm level rather than at portfolio level, parameter estimates are more stable due to better aggregation. Different from these previous studies, our estimation method involves neither aggregation nor portfolios.

In a similar vein, Belo, Xue, and Zhang (2013) estimate a q-model by matching average q at the firm level; Belo et al. (2022) target the entire time series of the mean valuation ratio of a portfolio to mitigate aggregation biases. We go one step further by targeting the entire panel of firm-level returns, thereby avoiding all three layers of aggregation — portfolio-level fundamentals, portfolio sorting, and time-series averaging of portfolio returns — that can bias parameter estimates in conventional approaches.[^5]

Section 4 reports parameter estimates and evaluates the overall fit of the model. Section 5 examines whether the model-implied fundamental returns generate the 12 anomaly premiums and their dynamics. Section 6 discusses the economic mechanisms behind the (in)capability of the estimated model in explaining anomalies. Section 7 concludes.

## 2. The Model

We adopt the two-capital model in Gonçalves, Xue, and Zhang (2020), in which firms use three inputs in production: long-term physical capital ($K$), short-term working capital ($W$), and a costlessly adjustable input ($S$) such as energy and purchased services, the prices of which are taken as given by firms. Operating profit of firm $i$ in industry $j$ at time $t$ is $\Pi_{it} = \Pi(K_{it}, W_{it}, S_{it})$, which exhibits constant returns to scale. Under the assumption of a perfectly competitive and frictionless market for input $S$, $S_{it}$ is chosen to maximize contemporaneous operating profits. With Cobb-Douglas production technology, the marginal products of physical and working capital are

$$\frac{\partial \Pi_{it}}{\partial K_{it}} = \gamma^{K}_{jt}\frac{Y_{it}}{K_{it}}, \qquad \frac{\partial \Pi_{it}}{\partial W_{it}} = \gamma^{W}_{jt}\frac{Y_{it}}{W_{it}},$$

in which $\gamma^{K}_{jt}$ and $\gamma^{W}_{jt}$ are the corresponding shares of capital in sales $Y_{it}$, with $\gamma_{jt} \equiv \gamma^{K}_{jt} + \gamma^{W}_{jt} < 1$. The model is estimated under four specifications, each with constant, industry-specific, time-varying, or industry-specific and time-varying parameter values. For generality, we formulate the model with industry-specific and time-varying parameter values in this section.

The implications of the model hold regardless of whether model parameters are constant, industry-specific, or time-varying, as long as they are exogenous. We follow the convention in this line of research and assume rational expectation in the model. The assumptions crucial to the model predictions are (1) the profit function $\Pi(K_{it}, W_{it}, S_{it})$ is constant-returns-to-scale; (2) the investment adjustment cost function is linearly homogeneous in investment and capital. In the estimations with time-varying parameters, we assume that these parameters follow random walks.[^6]

Firms choose investments in physical and working capital to maximize market equity. Physical capital evolves as $K_{it+1} = (1 - \delta_{it})K_{it} + I_{it}$, in which $I_{it}$ is the investment in physical capital, and $\delta_{it}$ is the depreciation rate. Investment in physical capital incurs quadratic adjustment costs:

$$\Phi_{it} \equiv \Phi(I_{it}, K_{it}) = \frac{a_{jt}}{2}\left(\frac{I_{it}}{K_{it}}\right)^{2} K_{it}, \tag{1}$$

where $a_{jt}$ is the physical adjustment cost parameter. Working capital evolves as $W_{it+1} = W_{it} + \Delta W_{it}$, in which $\Delta W_{it}$ is the investment in working capital. Working capital does not depreciate and is not accompanied by adjustment costs.

In addition to equity financing, firm $i$ in industry $j$ issues debt $B_{it+1}$ with interest rate $r^{B}_{it+1}$ at the beginning of time $t$, which is repaid at the beginning of $t+1$. At tax rate $\tau_t$, firm $i$'s net payout is $D_{it} \equiv (1 - \tau_t)(\Pi_{it} - \Phi_{it}) - I_{it} - \Delta W_{it} + B_{it+1} - r^{Ba}_{it}B_{it} + \tau_t\delta_{it}K_{it}$, in which $r^{Ba}_{it} \equiv r^{B}_{it} - \tau_t(r^{B}_{it} - 1)$ is the after-tax interest rate. Taking the stochastic pricing kernel $M_{t+1}$ as given, firm $i$ chooses $I_{it}, K_{it+1}, \Delta W_{it}, W_{it+1}$, and $B_{it+1}$ to maximize its cum-dividend market equity, $V_{it} \equiv E_t\!\left[\sum_{s=0}^{\infty} M_{t+s} D_{t+s}\right]$. The first-order condition for physical investment implies that $E_t[M_{t+1} r^{K}_{it+1}] = 1$, in which $r^{K}_{it+1}$ is the return on physical capital investment:

$$r^{K}_{it+1} = \frac{1-\tau_{t+1}}{1+(1-\tau_{t+1})a_{jt+1}}\left[\gamma^{K}_{jt+1}\frac{Y_{it+1}}{K_{it+1}} + \frac{a_{jt+1}}{2}\left(\frac{I_{it+1}}{K_{it+1}}\right)^{2}\right] + \tau_{t+1}\delta_{it+1} + (1-\delta_{it+1})\frac{I_{it}/K_{it}}{1+(1-\tau_{t})a_{jt}}. \tag{2}$$

Similarly, the first-order condition for working capital investment implies that $E_t[M_{t+1} r^{W}_{it+1}] = 1$, in which $r^{W}_{it+1}$ is the return on working capital investment:

$$r^{W}_{it+1} = 1 + (1-\tau_{t+1})\gamma^{W}_{jt+1}\frac{Y_{it+1}}{W_{it+1}}. \tag{3}$$

The weighted average of the two investment returns equals the weighted average cost of equity and the after-tax cost of debt:

$$w^{K}_{it} r^{K}_{it+1} + (1-w^{K}_{it}) r^{W}_{it+1} = w^{B}_{it} r^{Ba}_{it+1} + (1-w^{B}_{it}) r^{S}_{it+1}, \tag{4}$$

in which $w^{B}_{it} \equiv B_{it+1}/(V_{it} - D_{it} + B_{it+1})$ is the firm's market leverage, $r^{S}_{it+1} \equiv V_{it+1}/(V_{it} - D_{it})$ is the stock return, $w^{K}_{it} \equiv q^{K}_{it} K_{it+1}/(q^{K}_{it} K_{it+1} + W_{it+1})$ is the weight of firm's market value attributed to physical capital, and $q^{K}_{it} \equiv 1 + a_{jt}(1-\tau_t)I_{it}/K_{it}$ is the marginal q of physical capital. The marginal q of working capital is one in the absence of adjustment costs in working capital investment. The Tobin's q of firm $i$ at time $t$ is the weighted average of marginal q's of physical and working capitals:

$$q_{it} = \frac{P_{it}+B_{it+1}}{K_{it+1}+W_{it+1}} = \left[1+a_{jt}(1-\tau_t)\frac{I_{it}}{K_{it}}\right]\frac{K_{it+1}}{K_{it+1}+W_{it+1}} + \frac{W_{it+1}}{K_{it+1}+W_{it+1}}. \tag{5}$$

Solving for the stock return from equation (4) leads to the model-implied fundamental stock return of firm $i$ from $t$ to $t+1$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
r^{F}_{it+1} \equiv f(X_{it}, X_{it+1}\,|\,\theta_t, \theta_{t+1}) = \frac{1}{1-w^{B}_{it}}\Bigg\{& \frac{K_{it+1}}{K_{it+1}+W_{it+1}}\Bigg[\frac{1-\tau_{t+1}}{1+(1-\tau_{t+1})a_{jt+1}}\Big(\gamma_{jt+1}\tfrac{Y_{it+1}}{K_{it+1}}+\tfrac{a_{jt+1}}{2}\big(\tfrac{I_{it+1}}{K_{it+1}}\big)^{2}\Big)+\tau_{t+1}\delta_{it+1} \\
& \quad +(1-\delta_{it+1})\frac{I_{it}/K_{it}}{1+(1-\tau_t)a_{jt}}\Bigg] + \frac{W_{it+1}}{K_{it+1}+W_{it+1}}\Big[1+(1-\tau_{t+1})\gamma_{jt+1}\tfrac{Y_{it+1}}{W_{it+1}}\Big] - w^{B}_{it}\,r^{Ba}_{it+1}\Bigg\},
\end{aligned} \tag{6}
$$

where $X_{it}$ is the set of accounting variables used in equation (6) that represent firm $i$'s fundamentals, and $\theta_t \equiv \{(\gamma_{jt}, a_{jt});\, j = 1,\dots,10\}$ is the set of model parameters at time $t$ for the Fama–French 10 industries. The equality between the realized stock return and the model-implied fundamental return, $r^{S}_{it+1} = r^{F}_{it+1}$, holds for any firm $i$ and for any period from $t$ to $t+1$ under this framework. Next, we estimate the two structural parameters, $\gamma$ and $a$, based on this equality. Notice that $\gamma^{K}$ and $\gamma^{W}$ cannot be separately identified because $r^{F}$ depends on their summation only.

## 3. Data and Estimation Methodology

In this section, we first explain how to construct the firm characteristics used in equation (6) and the 120 decile portfolios, 10 for each anomaly. We then introduce the Bayesian MCMC estimation and compare its performance with those of GMM and NLS using simulation studies.

### 3.1 Data

Our sample includes all common stocks traded on NYSE, Amex, and NASDAQ with available accounting and return data. We exclude firms with primary SIC between 6000 and 6999 (financial firms), firms with negative book equity, and firms with nonpositive total assets, net property, plant, and equipment, or sales at the portfolio formation. These data items are needed to calculate firm-level fundamental returns. We obtain monthly stock return data from the Center for Research in Security Prices (CRSP). Firm-level accounting data are obtained from the annual and quarterly Standard & Poor's Compustat industrial files. Our data sample covers the period from January 1967 to June 2017.

#### 3.1.1 Anomalies

We explore 12 anomalies covering all six categories defined in Hou, Xue, and Zhang (2020): the value anomaly sorted on book-to-market equity ratio (BM); the momentum anomaly sorted on the prior 11-month returns skipping the most recent month (R11); four investment anomalies sorted on asset growth (I/A), net stock issues (NSI), investment-to-assets ratio (∆PI/A), and accruals (Accruals); three profitability anomalies sorted on return-on-equity (ROE), return-on-assets (ROA), and gross profitability (GP/A); two intangibles anomalies sorted on R&D expense-to-market ratio (RD/M) and advertising expense-to-market ratio (Ad/M); and one trading frictions anomaly sorted on market capitalization (Size).[^7] We choose these 12 anomalies based on two criteria: (1) the average value-weighted returns of their high-minus-low deciles with NYSE breakpoints are significant at the 5% level, with the exception of size, which is included since it is one of the most studied anomalies; (2) investment-based asset pricing models have been suggested by prior studies to explain these anomalies. Appendix A provides the definitions of these variables and the construction of the corresponding decile portfolios. Table 1 presents the monthly average excess returns of the 10 decile portfolios sorted on each of the 12 anomaly variables. We confirm that all 12 anomalies except size have statistically and economically significant premiums in our sample period.

[^7]: Hou, Xue, and Zhang (2020) show that almost all well-known anomalies in the trading frictions category cannot be successfully replicated. For completeness, we include the Size anomaly from this category since it is one of the most widely studied anomalies in the literature.

#### 3.1.2 Measures and timing alignment

Model-implied fundamental returns are constructed at annual frequency because the needed fundamental variables such as investments are only available at annual frequency for the long sample starting from 1967. In the model, time-$t$ stock variables are at the beginning of year $t$, and time-$t$ flow variables are over the course of year $t$. Thus, time-$t$ stock variables are obtained from the balance sheet of fiscal year $t-1$ and flow variables from the balance sheet of fiscal year $t$.

We adopt the same measures used by Gonçalves, Xue, and Zhang (2020) for the variables needed to construct the fundamental returns. Specifically, output $Y_{it}$ is measured as sales (Compustat annual item SALE). Physical capital $K_{it}$ is net property, plant, and equipment (item PPENT). Short-term working capital $W_{it}$ is current assets (item ACT). Total debt $B_{it+1}$ is long-term debt (item DLTT, zero if missing) plus short-term debt (item DLC, zero if missing) from the fiscal year-$t$ balance sheet. Tax rate $\tau$ is the statutory corporate income tax rate from the Commerce Clearing House's annual publications. The depreciation rate of physical capital, $\delta_{it}$, is the amount of depreciation and amortization (item DP) minus the amortization of intangibles (item AM, zero if missing) divided by physical capital (item PPENT). Physical investment, $I_{it}$, is measured as $K_{it+1} - (1-\delta_{it})K_{it}$. The market leverage $w^{B}_{it}$ is the ratio of total debt to the sum of total debt and market equity. The pre-tax cost of debt, $r^{B}_{it}$, is the ratio of total interest and related expenses (item XINT) scaled by total debt $B_{it}$. Following Gonçalves, Xue, and Zhang (2020), we winsorize unbounded variables ($I_{it}/K_{it}$, $I_{it+1}/K_{it+1}$, $\Delta W_{it}/W_{it}$, $\Delta W_{it+1}/W_{it+1}$) at the 2.5%–97.5% level; variables bounded below by zero ($Y_{it+1}/K_{it+1}$, $Y_{it+1}/W_{it+1}$, $Y_{it+1}/(K_{it+1}+W_{it+1})$, $W_{it+1}/K_{it+1}$, $\delta_{it+1}$, $r^{B}_{it+1}$) at the 0%–95% level; and do not winsorize variables bounded between zero and one (such as $K_{it+1}/(K_{it+1}+W_{it+1})$ or $w^{B}_{it}$). Summary statistics and the correlation matrix of these variables are reported in Table 2 and closely match those in Gonçalves, Xue, and Zhang (2020).

In the model, the fundamental stock return of firm $i$ from year $t$ to $t+1$, $r^{F}_{it+1}$, is constructed with both stock and flow variables at annual frequency. In the estimation, we match $r^{F}_{it+1}$ with the observed annual return of firm $i$ from the middle of fiscal year $t$ to the middle of fiscal year $t+1$. Specifically, if firm $i$'s fiscal end of year $t$ is month $l$, $r^{S}_{it+1}$, the counterpart of $r^{F}_{it+1}$, is the realized 12-month return between month $l-5$ and $l+6$.

To study anomalies, we construct fundamental portfolio returns based on fundamental firm-level returns. Even though firm-level fundamental returns change annually (in fiscal year), fundamental portfolio returns change monthly because fiscal year-endings vary across firms and portfolio compositions can also change monthly. To better align the timing and make a fair comparison, we follow Gonçalves, Xue, and Zhang (2020) and compound the realized portfolio stock returns within a 12-month rolling window with the month in question in the middle. Applying this rolling procedure to the realized monthly portfolio returns (January 1967 to June 2017) yields the monthly observations of annualized portfolio returns from June 1967 to December 2016. We validate our data construction and portfolio formation by successfully reproducing the realized and predicted returns (by the baseline model) of the BM, R11, I/A, and ROE deciles in Gonçalves, Xue, and Zhang (2020) using their estimated parameters (Figure A.1, which replicates Panel B of Figure 3 in their paper).

### 3.2 Estimation methodology

Prior studies (Liu, Whited, and Zhang, 2009; Gonçalves, Xue, and Zhang, 2020, among others) use the Generalized Method of Moments (GMM) to match the unconditional moments derived from equation (6): $E_T[r^{S}_{pt+1} - r^{F}_{pt+1}] = 0$ for testing portfolio $p$, where $E_T[\cdot]$ refers to the operation of taking the time-series average. To completely avoid portfolio dependence in parameter estimates, we instead target the entire panel of firm-level stock returns using the Bayesian Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) method. The Bayesian MCMC method can efficiently extract a large amount of information from firm-level stock returns and enables us to allow the parameters to vary across industries and over time. We consider four specifications: constant parameters, industry variations only, time variations only, and industry-and-time variations. Next, we explain our methodology in detail for the specification that allows both industry and time variations.

#### 3.2.1 Bayesian MCMC

Denote the technology parameter in the production function for industry $j$ at time $t$ as $\gamma_{jt}$ and the physical adjustment cost parameter as $a_{jt}$. The time series of parameter values are referred to as "latent variables" in Bayesian MCMC estimation and are assumed to evolve as random walk processes:

$$
\begin{pmatrix} \gamma_{jt+1} \\ a_{jt+1} \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \gamma_{jt} \\ a_{jt} \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} \sigma_\gamma & 0 \\ 0 & \sigma_a \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} e^{\gamma}_{jt+1} \\ e^{a}_{jt+1} \end{pmatrix}, \tag{7}
$$

where $e^{\gamma}_{jt+1}$ and $e^{a}_{jt+1}$ follow standard normal distributions independently, and $\sigma_\gamma$ and $\sigma_a$ are the conditional standard deviations of latent variables $\gamma_{jt+1}$ and $a_{jt+1}$ conditioning on the previous time $t$. Imposing a random walk process on the deep parameters not only encourages persistence, but also enables us to borrow information across time in estimation, leading to more efficient estimates. For the specification with time variations only, the same random walk process is assumed for all industries, and for specifications with no time variations, $e^{\gamma}_{jt+1}$ and $e^{a}_{jt+1}$ are set to zero.

The realized stock return of firm $i$ (in industry $j$) at time $t+1$ is modeled as the corresponding fundamental return plus an estimation error:

$$r^{S}_{it+1} = r^{F}_{it+1} + \varpi_{it}^{-1/2}\sigma_r e^{r}_{it+1}, \tag{8}$$

where $e^{r}_{it+1}$ follows the standard normal distribution, $\sigma_r$ is a parameter to be estimated, and the weight $\varpi_{it}^{-1/2}$ in the estimation error is specified as:

$$\varpi_{it} \equiv \frac{V_{it}}{\sum_{i=1}^{N_{jt}} V_{it}}, \tag{9}$$

in which $N_{jt}$ is the number of firms at time $t$ in industry $j$ to which firm $i$ belongs. By this specification, we introduce heteroskedasticity into the estimation errors of realized stock returns: the variance of a firm's estimation errors decreases with its market equity $V_{it}$, accommodating the fact that stock returns of large firms are less noisy and more reflective of their fundamentals.[^8] This makes the estimated model economically relevant in capturing the regularity of the majority of the economy. The same rationale motivates the use of NYSE breakpoints in constructing portfolios and weighted-least-squares regressions in asset pricing studies (e.g., Hou, Xue, and Zhang, 2015).

[^8]: Large firms have more analysts following than small firms, thus their value is under much closer scrutiny (Bhushan, 1989). Moreover, stocks of large firms are generally more liquid and their market values are less likely to be manipulated or affected by a small group of investors (Amihud, 2002).

For the MCMC method, prior distributions of the model parameters need to be specified. We use inverse gamma distributions for the priors of variances: $\sigma^{2}_{\gamma} \sim IG(\kappa^{\gamma}_1, \kappa^{\gamma}_2)$, $\sigma^{2}_{a} \sim IG(\kappa^{a}_1, \kappa^{a}_2)$, and $\sigma^{2}_{r} \sim IG(\kappa^{r}_1, \kappa^{r}_2)$, where $\kappa_1$ and $\kappa_2$ are the shape-scale hyper-parameters. The values of $\kappa^{r}_1, \kappa^{\gamma}_1, \kappa^{a}_1$ are 0.01, 1, and 1, respectively; the values of $\kappa^{r}_2, \kappa^{\gamma}_2, \kappa^{a}_2$ are 0.02, 5, and 5, respectively. The values of $\kappa_1$ are chosen relatively small so that the information from data is more likely to dominate; the values of $\kappa_2$ are set relatively large so that the variances of the priors are large and thus less informative. Although these choices are seemingly arbitrary, as MCMC runs and information from the data enters the posterior draws, these hyper-parameters weigh less and less; the information from data dominates the posterior draws when MCMC converges.

Finally, the time series of latent variables $\theta \equiv \{\theta_t;\, t = 1,\dots,T\}$, where $\theta_t = \{(\gamma_{jt}, a_{jt});\, j = 1,\dots,10\}$, and variance parameters $\sigma \equiv \{\sigma^{2}_\gamma, \sigma^{2}_a, \sigma^{2}_r\}$ are drawn iteratively from each complete conditional posterior distribution, yielding posterior samples from the joint posterior distribution. Based on equations (7) and (8), the joint posterior distribution of $\theta$ and $\sigma$ can be written (in proportional form) as:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(\theta, \sigma\,|\,X, r^{S}, r^{Ba}) \propto\ & \prod_{t=0}^{T-1}\prod_{i=1}^{N_{t+1}} \mathcal{N}\!\left(r^{S}_{it+1};\, r^{F}_{it+1},\, \sigma^{2}_r \varpi_{it}^{-1}\right) \times \prod_{t=0}^{T-1}\prod_{j=1}^{N_d} \mathcal{N}(\gamma_{jt+1};\, \gamma_{jt},\, \sigma^{2}_\gamma) \\
& \times \prod_{t=0}^{T-1}\prod_{j=1}^{N_d} \mathcal{N}(a_{jt+1};\, a_{jt},\, \sigma^{2}_a) \times IG(\sigma^{2}_r;\, \kappa^{r}_1, \kappa^{r}_2) \times IG(\sigma^{2}_\gamma;\, \kappa^{\gamma}_1, \kappa^{\gamma}_2) \times IG(\sigma^{2}_a;\, \kappa^{a}_1, \kappa^{a}_2),
\end{aligned} \tag{10}
$$

where $N_{t+1}$ is the number of firms at time $t+1$, $N_d$ is the number of industries, and $r^{F}_{it+1}$ is defined in (6). We run 20,000 MCMC iterations and use the last 5,000 iterations to obtain posterior draws, and confirm convergence of the posterior distributions.

#### 3.2.2 Comparison of Bayesian MCMC with GMM and NLS

Our MCMC estimation approach is fundamentally different from the estimation method in Liu, Whited, and Zhang (2009) and Gonçalves, Xue, and Zhang (2020), and it offers several advantages. **First**, our estimates of parameter values are independent of any specific testing portfolios: we utilize the entire distribution of firm-level stock returns, while GMM matches the time-series averages of returns on testing portfolios — critical for addressing Campbell's (2017) critique. **Second**, our MCMC algorithm generates random draws of model parameters from their joint posterior distribution given the observations, while GMM outputs deterministic point estimates; the Bayesian approach allows probabilistic inference for the estimated parameters and fundamental returns. **Third**, given the vast amount of firm-level information exploited, we can accurately identify the true parameter values even when they vary across industries and over time. With the Fama–French 10-industry classification, we estimate 2 × 500 latent variables for the 1967–2016 sample; although this is small relative to the 136,598 observations used, it is extremely difficult to estimate via GMM or maximum likelihood, so prior studies generally assume time-invariant parameters.

Lastly, the frequentist Nonlinear Least Squares (NLS) can also estimate industry-specific and time-varying parameters using firm-level stock returns:

$$\hat\theta^{NLS}_{jt+1} = \arg\min_{\theta_{jt+1}} \sum_{i=1}^{N_{jt+1}} \varpi_{it}\left[f\!\left(X_{it}, X_{it+1}\,\big|\,\hat\theta^{NLS}_{jt}, \theta_{jt+1}\right) - r^{S}_{it+1}\right]^{2}, \tag{11}$$

where $N_{jt+1}$ is the number of firms in industry $j$ at time $t+1$, $\hat\theta^{NLS}_{jt}$ is the estimated parameters for industry $j$ at $t$, and $\varpi_{it}$ is proportional to market equity as defined in (9). However, only the information of industry $j$ at time $t$ identifies $\hat\theta^{NLS}_{jt+1}$. In contrast, the posterior of $\theta_{jt}$ in Bayesian MCMC utilizes the information of the entire data sample: (i) the random walk in (7) connects information across time; (ii) $a_{jt}$ enters the probability distributions of both $r_{it}$ and $r_{it+1}$; and (iii) firm entry/exit and industry switching connect information across industries.

Simulation studies show that Bayesian MCMC performs very well for our highly nonlinear model and is able to discover the true parameter values under all four specifications, while NLS often fails to discover time-varying true values. We also conduct extensive simulations investigating performance under model misspecification; in neither case do we find GMM outperform Bayesian MCMC in terms of estimation bias. We suspect that, given the highly nonlinear relation between return and fundamentals, averaging returns as in GMM is unlikely to eliminate misspecification errors.

## 4. Parameter Estimation and Performance Evaluation

We conduct estimations of four model specifications: constant parameters, industry-specific parameters, time-varying parameters, and industry-specific and time-varying parameters. We compare our estimates with those in Gonçalves, Xue, and Zhang (2020), discuss whether the estimated parameter values make economic sense, and compare the performance of the four specifications in matching firm-level stock returns.

### 4.1 Parameter estimates

Our estimation generates the posterior distributions of the marginal product parameter $\gamma$ and the physical investment adjustment cost parameter $a$. For each estimate we report its posterior mean and credible interval (CI), the interval wherein a posterior draw of the parameter falls with a specified probability.[^9] Panel A of Table 3 shows that under constant parameters, the posterior mean of $\gamma$ is 0.15 with a CI of [0.15, 0.15], and the posterior mean of $a$ is 0.14 with a CI of [0.13, 0.14]. These narrow CIs indicate that the parameters are identified with high precision due to the simple model structure (only two structural parameters) and the large amount of information (136,598 firm-level observations).

[^9]: Formally, the posterior credible interval $I_p$ of parameter $\theta$ satisfies $P(\theta \in I_p\,|\,X, r^{S}, r^{Ba}) = p$. A credible interval is the Bayesian counterpart of the frequentist confidence interval: confidence intervals treat the parameter as fixed and the bounds as random variables, whereas credible intervals treat the parameter as a random variable with bounds determined by the posterior distribution.

Several observations emerge from Table 3. **First**, parameters are accurately estimated with narrow CIs even when they vary over time and across industries, highlighting that Bayesian MCMC extracts a large amount of information from firm-level returns. **Second**, allowing time variation is crucial for unbiased estimates when the underlying model is nonlinear in these parameters. When time variation is shut down, the estimates of $\gamma$ (Panels A and B) are close to the time-series averages of their time-varying counterparts (Panels C and D); in contrast, we see large changes in the estimates of $a$. For example, the posterior mean of $\gamma$ for Business Equipment is 0.23 in Panel B, the same as its time-series average in Panel D; however, the posterior mean of $a$ for Business Equipment is 0.78 in Panel B, less than half of its time-series average of 1.78 in Panel D. The reason is that the model-implied return is a linear function of $\gamma$ but a nonlinear function of $a$, as shown in equation (6). If the true value is time-varying, aggregating information along the time dimension under a nonlinear relation often generates an estimate far from the time-series average.

**Third**, the parameter estimates differ greatly across industries. For example, under industry-and-time variations (Panel D), $\gamma$ is 0.08 on average (CI [0.07, 0.09]) for the Wholesale & Retail sector, compared to 0.28 for Telecom — consistent with capital being less important for wholesale & retail than for telecom. The average posterior mean of $a$ ranges from 0.25 for Utilities to 1.78 for Business Equipment. Business Equipment has the highest marginal q of physical capital ($q^K = 1.53$) while Utilities has the lowest ($q^K = 1.02$), consistent with high-tech firms having the highest growth potential and regulated utilities the lowest. Overall, Table 3 shows large variations in parameter values across industries and over time; Bayesian MCMC is a useful tool for such scenarios. The value of $a$ turns out to be critical for the model to generate the value premium, discussed in detail in Section 6.

### 4.2 Overall fit of the estimation

Table 4 reports the posterior means and 95% credible intervals of the mean, standard deviation, skewness, kurtosis, the time-series average of cross-sectional correlations between fundamental and realized stock returns, and the mean absolute error (m.a.e.). The m.a.e. is defined as:

$$\text{m.a.e.} \equiv \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\frac{1}{N_{t+1}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{t+1}} \left| r^{S}_{it} - r^{F}_{it} \right|, \tag{12}$$

where $N_{t+1}$ is the number of firms in period $t+1$.

Several observations emerge from Table 4. **First**, the mean, skewness, and kurtosis of fundamental returns match well with those of realized returns across all four specifications. The mean of fundamental stock returns ranges from 14.97% to 15.65%, compared to 14.45% in the data; skewness ranges from 1.66 to 2.12, compared to 2.15; kurtosis ranges from 10.66 to 13.33, compared to 11.05. **Second**, the standard deviations of fundamental returns are much smaller than that of realized ones. The specification with industry-and-time-varying parameters generates the highest standard deviation, 34.17%, compared to 60.78% in the data, while industry-specific parameters generate the lowest, 18.49%. **Third**, the average cross-sectional correlation between realized and fundamental firm-level returns is highest for the industry-and-time-varying specification (0.20) and lowest for constant parameters (0.09). Lastly, the m.a.e. is lowest for the industry-and-time-varying specification (40.10%), followed by time-varying (40.85%), industry-specific (41.85%), and constant (42.45%). Figure 1 plots the histograms of realized (blue) and fundamental (orange) firm-level returns; realized returns have a wider distribution at both tails, and both distributions have longer right tails (positive skewness, kurtosis > 3).

### 4.3 Performance comparison of the four specifications

We evaluate each specification based on the m.a.e. of fundamental firm-level stock returns. Unlike GMM, Bayesian MCMC offers a probabilistic view: each posterior draw of $\theta$ leads to a panel of fundamental returns, so we obtain posterior distributions of the m.a.e. We test whether the differences in m.a.e. are statistically significant using:

$$d_a = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\frac{1}{N_{t+1}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{t+1}}\left(\left|r^{S}_{it+1} - r^{F}_{(a)\,it+1}\right| - \left|r^{S}_{it+1} - r^{F}_{(b)\,it+1}\right|\right), \tag{13}$$

where $r^{F}_{(b)}$ is the fundamental return under the benchmark specification (the one with the lowest m.a.e., i.e., industry-and-time-varying) and $r^{F}_{(a)}$ is under an alternative specification. Although similar in form to Diebold and Mariano (1995), the statistic differs in statistical properties; taking advantage of MCMC, we can still make valid inference. We record $d_a^{(m)}$ for the $m$-th posterior draw; the 5,000 draws provide the empirical distribution of $d_a$.

Figure 2 plots the distribution of $d_a$ for the three alternative specifications. The posterior distributions all lie in the positive region. None of the three 95% credible intervals — [1.80, 1.90], [1.22, 1.32], and [0.57, 0.67] for the constant, industry-specific, and time-varying specifications — includes zero, indicating that the industry-and-time-varying specification performs significantly better. Moreover, the model with time-varying parameters is closest to the benchmark, followed by industry-specific and constant parameters. In sum, the industry-and-time-varying specification has significantly better performance, and time variation is more crucial than industry variation.

## 5. Fundamental Anomalies

In this section, we examine whether the model-implied fundamental returns exhibit the same return regularities — in terms of average anomaly premiums and their time-series dynamics — when the model parameters are estimated to match firm-level stock returns. We focus on a set of well-documented anomalies based on the replication study by Hou, Xue, and Zhang (2020), who replicate 452 anomalies classified into six categories: momentum, value versus growth, investment, profitability, intangibles, and trading frictions. For a sample period close to ours (June 1967 to December 2017), only 153 anomalies can be successfully replicated.[^10] We then choose 12 anomalies, covering all six categories, that are most widely studied and can be successfully replicated in our sample period, and that investment-based asset pricing models have been suggested to explain.

[^10]: The replication of an anomaly is successful if the average return of its high-minus-low decile is significant at the 5% threshold based on portfolio sorts with NYSE breakpoints and value-weighted returns.

### 5.1 Anomaly premiums

Figure 3 plots the posterior distributions of the 12 fundamental factor premiums under the industry-and-time-varying specification and labels the 2.5, 50, and 97.5 percentiles. For example, the posterior distribution for "BM" indicates that, given the observed accounting variables and provided the model is correctly specified, the fundamental value premium per annum falls between 0.31% and 0.60% with 95% probability, with a posterior median of 0.46%. The posterior distributions are close to normal, indicating good convergence, and the credible intervals are extremely narrow — reflecting the simplicity of the model and the richness of firm-level information.

Table 5 presents the fundamental (value-weighted) factor premiums and alphas, defined as the difference between realized and fundamental factor premiums, $\alpha \equiv r^{S} - r^{F}$, of the high-minus-low hedging portfolios for each of the 12 anomalies under the four specifications. The results show that the industry-and-time-varying specification ($\theta_{jt}$) has the best performance in generating factor premiums consistent with the data, followed by industry-specific ($\theta_j$), time-varying ($\theta_t$), and constant ($\theta$) parameters. Specifically, all four specifications generate significant (at least at the 10% level) momentum (R11), profitability (ROE, GP/A, ROA), size, investment (NSI and ∆PI/A), and advertising (Ad/M) premiums with the correct signs. However, without industry variations, the model cannot generate a significant RD/M premium; both industry and time variations are needed for a significant I/A premium; and none of the specifications can generate a significant value premium or the accruals anomaly with the correct sign.

A significant alpha indicates that the fundamental factor premium differs significantly from the realized one in magnitude. Specification $\theta_{jt}$ fails to match the magnitudes of the I/A, NSI, GP/A, value, and accruals premiums. Overall, the estimated models under all specifications successfully generate significant momentum, size, profitability, and investment premiums. However, even the best-performing specification, $\theta_{jt}$, fails to generate a significant value premium. This contrasts sharply with Gonçalves, Xue, and Zhang (2020), whose estimation is based on the same model and same data sample as ours. The difference is that the ten value deciles are among the 40 testing portfolios in their estimation. We argue that this inclusion overstates the model's ability to explain these anomalies. Our results indicate that Campbell's (2017) critique remains a concern and underscores the importance of using portfolio-independent parameters to evaluate a model's capability in generating stock market anomalies.

### 5.2 Correlation between realized and fundamental portfolio returns

Table 6 reports the contemporaneous correlations between the realized and fundamental returns on the 120 decile portfolios and the 12 high-minus-low portfolios for the 12 anomalies. Not surprisingly, specification $\theta_{jt}$ performs the best: under it, the fundamental and realized portfolio returns are all highly correlated and the correlation coefficients are all significant at the 1% level. The average correlation is **0.69** for decile portfolios and **0.43** for high-minus-low portfolios.

The comparison among the four specifications indicates that both industry and time variations are critical to the high correlations for high-minus-low portfolios, while only time variation is critical for decile portfolios. Specifically, the average correlation of high-minus-low returns, $\bar\rho_{H-L}$, is 0.43 under $\theta_{jt}$; it drops to 0.18 under $\theta_j$ (time variation shut down), to 0.13 under $\theta_t$ (industry variation shut down), and to 0.12 under $\theta$ (both shut down). The fraction of positive and significant high-minus-low correlations drops from 100% under $\theta_{jt}$ to 67% under $\theta_j$, 58% under $\theta_t$, and 50% under $\theta$. At the same time, the average decile correlation $\bar\rho$ drops from 0.69 under $\theta_{jt}$ to 0.66 under $\theta_t$, to 0.06 under $\theta_j$, and to 0.03 under $\theta$; the fraction of positive and significant decile correlations drops from 100% under $\theta_{jt}$ and $\theta_t$ to 47.5% under $\theta_j$ and 42.5% under $\theta$.

Liu, Whited, and Zhang (2009) document a *correlation puzzle* — the contemporaneous correlations between realized and fundamental returns are weakly negative. Gonçalves, Xue, and Zhang (2020) show the correlation turns positive under their estimated model. Our estimated models generate higher correlations: among the 40 BM, R11, I/A, and ROE decile portfolios they study, 7 out of 40 correlations are positive and significant in Panel B of their Table 6, compared to 19 out of 40 under our specification $\theta$. With industry and time variations, our estimations yield even higher correlations. Therefore, the high correlations in Table 6 stem from the target of our estimation method — the entire sample of firm-level returns — and its flexibility to allow industry and time variations.

## 6. Discussions

The small estimated value of $a$ is the reason why our model fails to generate the value premium, whereas the model in Gonçalves, Xue, and Zhang (2020) successfully does. Although both use the same model and data sample and construct fundamental returns at the firm level, we estimate parameter values by matching each firm's annual stock returns for the entire sample from 1967 to 2016, while Gonçalves, Xue, and Zhang (2020) target the average returns of specific testing portfolios. Our estimated investment adjustment cost parameter $a$ is substantially smaller than theirs.[^11] For example, Gonçalves, Xue, and Zhang (2020) estimate $a = 2.84$ (std = 0.47) in their baseline GMM targeting the average returns of the value, momentum, I/A, and ROE decile portfolios. In contrast, our estimate under constant parameters is 0.14.

[^11]: Their parameter estimates vary with the testing portfolios under their baseline estimation method, ranging from 1.63 to 8.11 as shown in Panel B of Table 3 of Gonçalves, Xue, and Zhang (2020).

Table A.1 in the Internet Appendix shows that value firms have lower lagged investment-to-capital and sales-to-capital ratios than growth firms. At the same time, equation (6) implies a negative relation of stock return to $I_{it}/K_{it}$ but a positive relation to $Y_{it+1}/K_{it+1}$. Consequently, for the model to generate a positive and significant value premium, the investment adjustment cost parameter needs to be large enough. The question is: how reasonable are our estimates of $a$? And more importantly, are the low estimates of $a$ at odds with the values used in the investment-based asset pricing models to generate the value premium? We aim to answer these questions in this section. At the end, we also discuss possible reasons why the model fails to account for the accrual anomaly.

### 6.1 The value anomaly

#### 6.1.1 Values of $a$ in the literature to generate the value premium

Our model features a symmetric adjustment cost function to ensure Hayashi (1982) equality. Moreover, given that 92.02% (92.06%) of the observations in our sample are positive (non-negative) investments and the estimation is based on annual data, our estimate is essentially the parameter value for positive investment at annual frequency. Most theoretical models in the literature are calibrated (or estimated using SMM) at monthly frequency, with the exception of Tuzel (2010) and Li (2017). Panel A of Table A.11 in the Internet Appendix lists 13 theoretical articles in asset pricing published since 1999 that feature quadratic investment adjustment costs, with calibrated $a^+$ (positive investment) and $a^-$ (negative investment), all converted to annual values. A wide range of values have been used for $a^+$, with 0.00 and 0.08 at the low end and 25 at the high end.

For comparison, the baseline estimate of $a$ in Gonçalves, Xue, and Zhang (2020) is 2.84, on the higher end: only two papers (Bai et al., 2019; Herskovic, Kind, and Kung, 2023) use a value larger than theirs. Our estimate of $a$ is 0.14 under constant parameters, 0.19 on average under industry-specific parameters, 0.29 on average under time-varying parameters, and 0.727 on average under industry-and-time-varying parameters. These values lie on the low end of, but still within, the range used in the literature. Even for our lowest estimate (0.14), two papers (Li, 2017; Belo, Lin, and Yang, 2019) use lower values. Therefore, although our estimates are significantly lower than those in GXZ, they are within the range of values used in investment-based asset pricing models.

In a widely cited study with the sole purpose of estimating the adjustment cost parameter, Cooper and Haltiwanger (2006) estimate the quadratic adjustment cost parameter at 0.455 when only quadratic adjustment cost is present, dropping to 0.049 when nonconvex adjustment costs are added, by matching the serial correlation of investment rates, the correlation of profit shocks and investment rates, and positive/negative investment spike rates. Both estimates are closer to ours than to those in Gonçalves, Xue, and Zhang (2020). More importantly, using simulated data, Cooper and Haltiwanger (2006) show that due to measurement errors in average q, the coefficient on average q in investment-q regressions implies an estimate of $a$ a hundred times as large as its true value — explaining why prior estimates based on investment-q regressions generate extremely large estimates (e.g., 20 in Hayashi, 1982, and 3 in Gilchrist and Himmelberg, 1995). After correcting measurement error using instrumental variables, Hall (2004) finds estimates of $a$ close to zero and statistically insignificant. Thus, although our estimates of $a$ are lower than those in GXZ, they are comparable to others in the literature.

#### 6.1.2 Mechanism to generate the value premium

Asset pricing models that generate the value premium often rely on costly investment reversibility. Table A.11 lists 28 such theoretical articles published since 1999, all featuring costly reversibility — investment irreversibility, asymmetric nonconvex and/or convex adjustment costs, or a lower resale price of capital. Costly reversibility makes value firms riskier than growth firms during bad times, resulting in a higher risk premium for value firms. Therefore, the value premium is primarily driven by the large adjustment cost of *negative* investment (Zhang, 2005, among others).

Bai et al. (2023) estimate, via SMM, a model with asymmetric investment adjustment cost. In their Table S3, the estimated monthly cost parameter for positive investment is 0.1385 (an annual value of 0.012) and statistically insignificant, whereas the cost parameter for negative investment is 102.26 (annual value of 8.52) and highly significant. Under this set of parameters, the model generates a large and positive value premium; however, once the cost parameter for negative investment is lowered to the same value as for positive investment, the value premium becomes negative. Similarly, Favilukis and Lin (2016) calibrate a symmetric quarterly adjustment cost function with a parameter ranging from 0.3 (monthly 0.075) under Cobb-Douglas and show the model cannot generate the value premium with symmetric adjustment costs unless wage rigidity (operational leverage) is added.

In sum, our model is highly simplified, and we do not expect it to generate all anomalies. The central argument of our paper is that a model's ability to explain anomalies can be overstated if its parameters are selected to match the anomaly returns it aims to explain. The failure to generate the value premium when the average returns of the book-to-market deciles are not the target moments illustrates this precisely. Our results indicate that Campbell's (2017) critique continues to be a serious concern even if portfolio returns are aggregated from model-implied firm-level stock returns as in Gonçalves, Xue, and Zhang (2020).

### 6.2 The accruals anomaly

Table A.14 of the Internet Appendix shows that $Y_{it+1}/K_{it+1}$ is the most important driver of return spreads across firms sorted on accruals. According to Table A.1, high-accruals firms have higher $Y_{it+1}/K_{it+1}$ than low-accruals firms, which leads to a positive high-minus-low accruals spread — opposite to the one in the data.

However, differences in $Y_{it+1}/K_{it+1}$ between high- and low-accruals firms are likely overstated. The concept of accruals is absent in our model, and cash and accruals basis accountings are treated the same; the earnings of high- and low-accruals firms are assumed to have the same quality. High accruals mean high profitability in the model, which is not necessarily true in the data. Data shows that subsequent write-offs of accounts receivable happen more often to high-accruals firms (Dechow and Dichev, 2002). Therefore, the true difference in profitability is likely much smaller, which may lead to a smaller or even negative fundamental accruals premium.

Prior literature (Wu, Zhang, and Zhang, 2010; Zhang, 2007) argues that high-accruals firms have lower average returns due to their higher investment rate on working capital — analogous to the physical investment premium. Assuming working-capital investment incurs adjustment costs, a higher working-capital investment rate leads to a higher marginal cost and thus lower fundamental return. Our baseline assumes zero adjustment costs on working-capital investment for comparison with GXZ. We estimate an extended model with quadratic adjustment costs on working-capital investment (Section A.3 of the Internet Appendix); parameter estimates and fundamental anomaly premiums are reported in Tables A.12 and A.13. The results are quantitatively similar and the accruals premium stays positive and significant, indicating that the failure to generate the accruals premium is not due to the absence of working-capital adjustment costs.[^12] Overall, our evidence suggests that explicitly modeling earnings quality is a more promising direction to generate the accruals anomaly, which we leave for future research.

[^12]: Our estimates of the adjustment cost parameter on working-capital investment are close to those in Gonçalves, Xue, and Zhang (2020). When using their baseline parameter estimates for the extended model with working-capital adjustment costs (Section E of the Internet Appendix), we also find positive implied accruals premiums.

## 7. Conclusion

Can stock market anomalies be replicated within an investment-based asset pricing framework? To answer this question, prior studies often choose model parameters to fit the same set of anomaly returns that these studies aim to explain. In this paper, we propose a portfolio-independent estimation methodology based on Bayesian MCMC, which can be used to fairly evaluate the performance of any given model in explaining anomalies.

Applying this method to the same two-capital q-model as that in Gonçalves, Xue, and Zhang (2020) to match the entire sample of firm-level returns, our estimations yield higher correlations between realized and fundamental portfolio returns compared to prior literature. The estimated two-capital model also generates large and significant size, momentum, profitability, investment, and intangibles premiums.

However, we show that even under our best-fitting estimation with industry-specific and time-varying parameter values, the model fails to generate the value premium. Our results thus echo Campbell's (2017) critique and question the practice of evaluating a model's capability in generating certain anomaly premiums based on the set of model parameters chosen to match the corresponding decile portfolio returns.

## Appendix A. Definition of Sorting Variables

- **BM** (Davis, Fama, and French, 2000): Book-to-market equity ratio, defined as the book value of equity for fiscal year ending in the previous calendar year $t-1$ divided by the market value of equity at the end of December of year $t-1$. Book equity is stockholders' book equity, plus balance-sheet deferred taxes and investment tax credit (item TXDITC or the sum of TXDB and ITCB) if available, minus the book value of preferred stock. Stockholders' equity is the value reported by Compustat (item SEQ) if available; otherwise common equity (CEQ) plus the par value of preferred stock (PSTK), or total assets (AT) minus total liabilities (LT). Depending on availability, redemption (PSTKRV), liquidating (PSTKL), or par value (PSTK) is used for preferred stock.

- **R11** (Fama and French, 1996; Carhart, 1997): Prior 11-month returns from month $t-12$ to $t-2$.

- **I/A** (Cooper, Gulen, and Schill, 2008): Change in total assets (Compustat annual item AT) scaled by lagged total assets. At the end of June of year $t$, NYSE breakpoints split stocks into deciles based on I/A for the fiscal year ending in $t-1$; monthly value-weighted decile returns run from July of $t$ to June of $t+1$.

- **ROE** (Hou, Xue, and Zhang, 2020): Income before extraordinary items (Compustat quarterly item IBQ) divided by 1-quarter-lagged book equity. From 1972 onward, quarterly book equity is shareholders' equity plus deferred taxes (TXDITCQ) minus preferred stock (PSTKQ), using SEQQ, or CEQQ + PSTKQ, or ATQ − LTQ in that order. Prior to 1972, book equity is from Compustat annual files with quarterly imputation via clean-surplus accounting. At the start of each month $t$, stocks are sorted into deciles on their most recent ROE.

- **Size** (Fama and French, 1992): Price times shares outstanding from CRSP. At the end of June of year $t$, NYSE breakpoints sort stocks into deciles based on June-end Size; monthly value-weighted decile returns run from July of $t$ to June of $t+1$.

- **Accruals** (Sloan, 1996): Measured as $(\Delta ACT - \Delta CHE - \Delta LCT + \Delta DLC + \Delta TXP - \Delta DP)/[(AT + AT_{-1})/2]$, where $\Delta ACT$ is the annual change in total current assets, $\Delta CHE$ in cash and short-term investments, $\Delta LCT$ in current liabilities, $\Delta DLC$ in debt in current liabilities, $\Delta TXP$ in income taxes payable, $\Delta DP$ in depreciation and amortization, and $(AT + AT_{-1})/2$ is average total assets over the last two years. NYSE breakpoints sort stocks into deciles at the end of June of year $t$.

- **NSI** (Fama and French, 2008): Net stock issues, measured as the natural log of the ratio of split-adjusted shares outstanding (CSHO × AJEX) scaled by lagged split-adjusted shares outstanding.

- **∆PI/A** (Lyandres, Sun, and Zhang, 2008): Changes in gross property, plant, and equipment (PPEGT) plus changes in inventory (INVT), scaled by lagged total assets (AT).

- **GP/A** (Novy-Marx, 2013): Total revenue (REVT) minus cost of goods sold (COGS), divided by current total assets (AT).

- **ROA** (Balakrishnan, Bartov, and Faurel, 2010; Hou, Xue, and Zhang, 2020): Income before extraordinary items (quarterly IBQ) divided by 1-quarter-lagged total assets (ATQ). Stocks are sorted into deciles at the start of each month on ROA computed with the most recently announced quarterly earnings.

- **RD/M** (Chan, Lakonishok, and Sougiannis, 2001; Hou, Xue, and Zhang, 2020): R&D expenses (annual XRD) divided by market equity. Only firms with positive R&D expenses are kept; RD/M decile returns start in July 1976 (R&D accounting was standardized in 1975).

- **Ad/M** (Chan, Lakonishok, and Sougiannis, 2001; Hou, Xue, and Zhang, 2020): Advertising expenses (annual XAD) divided by market equity. Only firms with positive advertising expenses are kept; Ad/M decile returns start in July 1973 (sufficient XAD data start in 1972).

- **OCA and IOCA** (Eisfeldt and Papanikolaou, 2013; Hou, Xue, and Zhang, 2020): Organizational capital-to-assets ratio and industry-adjusted organizational capital-to-assets ratio. The stock of organization capital is constructed using the perpetual inventory method:

$$OC_{it} = (1-\delta)OC_{it-1} + SG\&A_{it}/CPI_{t}, \tag{A.1}$$

  where $OC_{it}$ is the organization capital of firm $i$ at the end of year $t$, $SG\&A_{it}$ is selling, general, and administrative expenses (Compustat annual item XSGA), $CPI_t$ is the consumer price index, and $\delta$ is the annual depreciation rate of OC. The initial stock of OC is:

$$OC_{i0} = SG\&A_{i0}/(g + \delta), \tag{A.2}$$

  where $SG\&A_{i0}$ is the first valid SG&A observation (zero or positive), $g$ is the long-term growth rate of SG&A (assumed 10%), and $\delta$ is the OC depreciation rate (assumed 15%). Missing SG&A values after the starting date are treated as zero. OCA is OC scaled by total assets. IOCA demeans a firm's OCA by its industry mean and divides by the within-industry standard deviation of OCA, using the Fama and French (1997) 17-industry classification; OCA is winsorized at the 1% and 99% levels each year before standardization.
