## Highlight

- **Alpha idea**: Revive the large set of return anomalies that have decayed since 2003 by overlaying standard characteristic-sorted long–short portfolios with a dependent double sort on machine-learning expected returns net of scale-dependent transaction costs, isolating the stocks for which the anomaly signal and the (cost-adjusted) expected-return estimate point the same way.
- **Data source(s)**: 153 firm characteristics from Jensen, Kelly, and Pedersen (2023), built on CRSP and Compustat; out-of-sample expected-return forecasts from the neural-network approach of Gu, Kelly, and Xiu (2020); price-impact calibration from Frazzini, Israel, and Moskowitz (2018); mutual-fund holdings from the CRSP Mutual Fund Database.
- **Universe**: U.S. equities (CRSP/Compustat) for the anomaly portfolios; mutual funds restricted to an equity share of at least 50% (holdings matched to the JKP sample) and a minimum of 10 matched stocks.
- **Sample period**: Neural-network training from January 1980; out-of-sample testing from January 2004 through December 2023.
- **Key finding**: A simple 1/N investment in anomalies that are *ex ante* expected to be implementable delivers out-of-sample net Sharpe ratios of 0.64–1.01 across fund sizes (micro to large), with highly significant CAPM α of 7.7%–20.3% per year and negative market beta. Mutual funds tilt toward implementable anomalies; a one-IQR increase in such alignment raises average fund returns by 8.48 percentage points per year (cross-sectionally) and 6.02 points (panel).
- **Signal construction**: A dependent double quintile sort — first by anomaly characteristic $c$, then within each quintile by expected net return $E_t[r^{net}_{i,t+1}] = E_t[r_{i,t+1}] - E_t[TC]$ — going long the high-$c$/high-$E[r]$ cell and short the low-$c$/low-$E[r]$ cell. $E_t[r_{i,t+1}]$ comes from a three-hidden-layer feed-forward neural network; $E_t[TC]$ comes from a quadratic, scale-dependent price-impact model calibrated so that trading 1% of daily volume moves price by ≈0.1%.
- **Revival mechanics**: Expected-return adjustment matters most for micro/small funds; transaction-cost adjustment matters most for medium/large funds. Quality- and accrual-based anomalies revive best across fund sizes; size-based anomalies cannot be revived for funds above micro size.

## Abstract

Prior literature finds that most anomalies fail to deliver significant returns, especially when considering implementation costs. We develop a framework to revive anomalies by incorporating expected returns and transaction costs into portfolio formation. Our approach identifies implementable anomalies across different fund sizes by (i) using machine learning return forecasts and (ii) accounting for scale-dependent price impacts. A 1/N investment strategy in implementable anomalies yields out-of-sample Sharpe ratios between 0.64 and 1.01 with highly significant alphas across all fund sizes. We further document that mutual funds tilt their holdings toward implementable anomalies and that such tilts predict higher subsequent fund returns.

**Keywords:** Asset Pricing, Factors, Trading Volume, Mutual Funds

**JEL classification:** G10, G11, G12

## 1. Introduction

In this paper, we revisit the recent performance of well-known return anomalies, like size, value, and momentum, which serve as the building block of many mutual funds and other investment vehicles. Academic evidence suggests that most anomalies have failed to deliver significant returns over the past twenty years (Green, Hand, and Zhang, 2017), especially when considering their implementation costs that can be prohibitive with high turnover rates (Chen and Velikov, 2023). Green et al. (2017) point to multiple reasons for the decay in anomaly performance, including a decrease in the level of mispricing due to regulatory changes. While we do not pin down a specific cause, we acknowledge that the decay coincides with the advent of more accurate methods to measure expected returns.

We develop a framework to address the declining performance of simple long–short anomalies by double-sorting portfolios based on the respective anomaly and expected returns net of transaction costs. To estimate expected returns, we follow Gu, Kelly, and Xiu (2020) and employ machine learning return forecasts based on the 153 stock characteristics documented in Jensen, Kelly, and Pedersen (2023). To quantify expected transaction costs, we differentiate between fund sizes and incorporate a scale-dependent price impact model, following Jensen, Kelly, Malamud, and Pedersen (2025) and Frazzini, Israel, and Moskowitz (2018). One major advantage of our framework is that it preserves the interpretability of standard factor portfolios while incorporating modern machine learning methods. Specifically, our approach serves as an overlay that identifies the most promising investment opportunities for each return factor across different fund sizes.

We start by showing that only a small number of anomalies produced significantly positive returns net of price impact costs for funds with varying assets under management (AUM) in an *ex post* analysis starting in 2004. The four fund sizes chosen (micro, small, medium, and large) are inspired by the distribution of fund sizes in the CRSP Mutual Fund Database, roughly reflecting the 20th, 40th, 60th, and 80th percentiles. The fund sizes are measured in 2023 and discounted back in time with the market return, following Jensen et al. (2025). For small funds managing 200mn USD, 10 of the 153 characteristic-based anomalies documented in Jensen et al. (2023) generated significantly positive net returns of 8% on average, with an average Sharpe ratio of 0.60. In hindsight, micro funds managing 40mn USD would have had 17 and medium-sized funds managing 500mn USD 3 anomalies with significant returns at their disposal. For large funds, managing assets in excess of 1.6bn USD, only a single anomaly generated significantly positive net returns over the past two decades.

We then show that incorporating next-month expected returns of the constituents into the formation of the anomaly portfolios substantially increases the number of anomalies with a significantly positive net return over the past 20 years. Our expected return measure uses stock characteristics as conditioning information and follows the neural network approach developed in Gu et al. (2020), which has shown great success in this domain across asset classes (Bali, Beckmeyer, Moerke, and Weigert, 2023). Specifically, we perform conditional quintile sorts, first by the anomaly portfolio's conditioning characteristic, and then by the stocks' expected returns. In the long leg of each anomaly, we retain only those stocks in the highest expected return quintile, whereas we retain only the stocks in the lowest expected return quintile for the short leg.[^1] For small funds, incorporating expected returns increases the number of significant anomalies to 73 with net returns of 18% and a Sharpe ratio of 0.70 on average. Micro funds would have had 126 and medium-sized funds 9 significant anomalies, if the selection of applicable stocks also considers their expected next-month return. For the largest funds, not a single anomaly's returns are significantly positive, even after this treatment.

[^1]: We show that this approach is vastly different from moving to finer quantiles, such as deciles, or ventiles.

To account for implementation costs, we estimate expected transaction costs using a scale-dependent price impact model, following Jensen et al. (2025) and Frazzini et al. (2018). We then adjust expected returns for the estimated price impact within our conditional quintile sorts. Once the portfolio formation considers expected returns and transaction costs of the included stocks, we find that small funds have access to 101 significant anomalies with net returns of 14% and a Sharpe ratio of 0.71 on average. Compared to the previous double sort based solely on expected returns, the number of significant anomalies does not change for micro funds and stays at 126. However, the joint consideration of expected returns and expected transaction costs yields a set of 81 significant anomalies for medium-sized funds and 41 for large funds. These findings indicate that our framework is effective across all fund sizes. Micro and small funds particularly benefit from our expected return extension, whereas expected transaction costs are most critical for medium and large funds due to their substantial price impact. In terms of anomaly themes, our approach exhibits strong performance for quality- and accrual-based strategies. Notably, we are unable to revive any size-based anomalies for fund sizes ranging from small to large, highlighting their dependence on illiquid, small-cap stocks.

Naturally, this historical analysis is silent on identifying the set of anomalies that are expected to generate positive returns in the future and therefore does not directly inform the construction of a systematic investment strategy. We therefore investigate the extent to which this *ex post* evidence translates into improved out-of-sample investment performance for hypothetical funds with varying AUM. In an *ex ante* analysis, we evaluate how many of the 153 characteristic-based anomalies are expected to yield positive returns net of transaction costs for a given fund size, which we define as **implementable anomalies**. A simple 1/N investment strategy based on implementable anomalies achieves economically meaningful net returns across micro, small, medium-sized, and large funds. For instance, a small fund generates average returns net of transaction costs of 12.0% over our 20-year out-of-sample period, with a Sharpe ratio of 0.90 and a CAPM α of 15.5% per year. Even a large fund achieves an average net return of 5.4% with a Sharpe ratio of 0.64 over the same period, alongside a highly significant CAPM α of 7.7%, indicating substantial opportunities relative to a market-only investment.

To assess the economic significance of our results, we combine information on which anomalies are expected to be implementable at a given fund's AUM with the fund's portfolio holdings from the CRSP mutual fund database at the fund-month level. We then introduce a novel measure, **anomaly alignment**, which captures the average overlap between a mutual fund's holdings and our anomaly portfolios adjusted for expected returns and transaction costs, for both implementable and non-implementable anomalies. Economically, a higher level of anomaly alignment indicates that a fund more closely tracks our augmented anomaly portfolios on average. We show that mutual funds across all fund sizes tilt their holdings toward implementable anomalies and away from non-implementable anomalies, suggesting that they indirectly incorporate expectations about returns and transaction costs into portfolio formation.

In a regression setting, we show that mutual funds whose holdings are more closely aligned with the set of implementable anomalies at their respective AUM levels generate substantially more value for investors, as reflected in significantly higher fund returns net of expenses. Cross-sectional regressions indicate that an interquartile range (IQR) increase in the average anomaly alignment of implementable anomalies raises the average monthly return by 0.71 percentage points, corresponding to an annual return differential of 8.48 percentage points. Panel regressions further show that a one IQR increase in current anomaly alignment of implementable anomalies increases fund returns in the subsequent month by 0.50 percentage points, which translates into an annualized difference of 6.02 percentage points. Notably, variation in anomaly alignment among non-implementable anomalies has no significant effect on fund returns in either cross-sectional or panel regressions. This finding provides empirical support for our prediction that mutual funds can exploit only those return anomalies that are implementable given their fund size.

Interestingly, the outperformance in net returns is not offset by higher management fees, suggesting that, on average, mutual funds do not charge investors for holding portfolios that are more closely aligned with implementable return anomalies. Neither cross-sectional nor panel regressions indicate a significant relationship between anomaly alignment of implementable anomalies and management fees. In contrast, mutual funds whose holdings are more closely aligned with non-implementable anomalies charge significantly lower management fees, suggesting that investors recognize the distinction between implementable and non-implementable anomalies for a given mutual fund. Cross-sectional regressions show that a one IQR increase in the average anomaly alignment of non-implementable anomalies is associated with a 0.08 percentage point decrease in the average annualized management fee. Panel regressions support this finding, indicating that a one IQR increase in current anomaly alignment of non-implementable anomalies is associated with a 0.05 percentage point decrease in annualized management fees in the subsequent month. This effect is economically meaningful, given that the average annualized management fee in the full sample is 0.44%.

**Related Literature.** We add to the literature discussing the decaying performance of return anomalies. McLean and Pontiff (2016) show that anomaly performance deteriorates after their publication in academic journals, and Green et al. (2017) document a general trend of decaying performance in return anomalies after 2003, with Chen and Velikov (2023) showing that anomalies fail to survive transaction costs in the form of effective spreads. DeMiguel, Martin-Utrera, Nogales, and Uppal (2020) show that the consideration of transaction costs changes the number of significant characteristics, and Jensen et al. (2025) estimate an "implementable" efficient frontier that directly depends on rebalancing costs. Using actual transactions of an institutional investor, Frazzini et al. (2018) show that effective transaction costs are well-explained by a quadratic price impact, which we use in this paper.

We also contribute to the literature that tries to understand the investment process of mutual funds. Closely related to our study, Patton and Weller (2020) estimate the all-in trading costs of popular return anomalies from the exposure of mutual funds, and Johansson, Sabbatucci, and Tamoni (2025) replicate anomalies with ETFs and mutual funds and show that the resulting returns are smaller than on paper.

Finally, we add to the broad literature that applies machine learning methods to measure expected returns (Gu et al., 2020; Bali et al., 2023). Similar to Avramov, Cheng, and Metzker (2023) and Jensen et al. (2025), we argue in favor of incorporating information about investment frictions and transaction costs when evaluating the efficacy of these methods in asset pricing, and link these considerations to the de facto decision-making process of investment professionals.

## 2. Methodology

Our approach is best understood as a dependent double sort. For this, at each point in time $t$, we sort stocks in a first step into quintile portfolios based on one of the 153 characteristics $c$ provided in the dataset of Jensen et al. (2023).[^2] From this exercise we obtain the value-weighted gross return of the high minus low portfolio sorted by characteristic $c$ at time $t$ and denote it by $r^{HL}_{c,t+1}$, with

[^2]: For this exercise we adhere to the sorting direction from the original study as compiled by Jensen et al. (2023).

$$
r^{HL}_{c,t+1} = r^{High}_{c,t+1} - r^{Low}_{c,t+1}, \tag{1}
$$

where $r^{High}_{c,t+1}$ and $r^{Low}_{c,t+1}$ are the value-weighted returns of the quintile portfolios with the highest and lowest values of characteristic $c$, respectively. While earlier studies found these anomaly characteristics to contain valuable investment advice in the sense that a hypothetical investor following the strategy outlined in Eq. (1) could have expected positive average risk-adjusted returns, those strategies appear to have ceased to deliver in the more recent past. Green et al. (2017) show that most characteristics fail to uncover valuable information for future returns after 2003, an effect that is distinct from the post-publication decay discussed in McLean and Pontiff (2016). Furthermore, Chen and Velikov (2023) show that most remaining characteristics with a significant gross return fail to deliver once transaction costs and fees are taken into account.

The second step in our methodology attempts to revive these anomalies by incorporating an estimate of expected net returns into the sorting procedure. Specifically, suppose we have an estimate of the expected future net return for stock $i$, which we denote by $E_t[r^{net}_{i,t+1}]$. Within each quintile portfolio formed on characteristic $c$, we sort stocks again according to $E_t[r^{net}_{i,t+1}]$ into quintile portfolios. This procedure yields a set of 25 portfolios that are formed through dependent sorting. Our trading strategy focuses on the extreme portfolios implied by this double sorting procedure. On the long side, we select the portfolio that contains stocks with the highest values of $E_t[r^{net}_{i,t+1}]$ within the quintile of stocks with the highest values of characteristic $c$. On the short side, we select the portfolio that contains the stocks with the lowest expected net returns within the quintile of stocks with the lowest values of characteristic $c$. The return of the resulting strategy therefore reflects the performance difference between these two portfolios. Formally, let $r^{High,High}_{c,t+1}$ denote the value-weighted return of the portfolio of stocks with both the highest values of characteristic $c$ and the highest values of $E_t[r^{net}_{i,t+1}]$. Analogously, $r^{Low,Low}_{c,t+1}$ denotes the value-weighted return of the portfolio of stocks with both the lowest values of characteristic $c$ and the lowest values of $E_t[r^{net}_{i,t+1}]$. The return of our double-sorted strategy, $r^{HL,Revived}_{c,t+1}$, is then given by

$$
r^{HL,Revived}_{c,t+1} = r^{High,High}_{c,t+1} - r^{Low,Low}_{c,t+1}. \tag{2}
$$

This construction isolates stocks that simultaneously exhibit extreme values along both sorting dimensions. The long leg consists of stocks with high values of characteristic $c$ that are also assigned high expected net returns, whereas the short leg consists of stocks with low characteristic values and low expected net returns. By conditioning the anomaly portfolio on expected net returns, the strategy concentrates positions in stocks for which the anomaly signal and the expected net return estimate point in the same direction.

There are two key benefits of this approach. First, the dependent double sort ensures diversification of the final portfolios, as the number of stocks in each portfolio depends only on the size of the stock universe and the number of target portfolios. Second, irrespective of how the expected return signal, $E_t[r^{net}_{i,t+1}]$, is obtained, the resulting portfolio maintains the interpretability of a characteristic-sorted portfolio. This feature is particularly important for asset managers who are required to communicate their investment strategies to potential investors. While we will demonstrate how machine learning methods can be used to obtain estimates of expected future net returns, the portfolio construction framework mitigates some of the concerns related to the opaque nature of such models. Because the final portfolios remain organized along familiar characteristic dimensions, the investment strategy retains a transparent economic interpretation even when the underlying expected return estimates are generated using complex predictive models. In the empirical analysis in Section 4, we show that mutual funds can, and likely do, exploit this mechanism to construct superior portfolios while maintaining the appearance of traditional characteristic-based investment strategies.

Having established the double sorting framework, we next describe how expected gross returns, $E_t[r_{i,t+1}]$, are estimated for each stock. In our setting, these estimates are obtained using machine learning methods and are part of the second sorting dimension of the strategy. Transaction costs ($TC$) are considered separately and enter additively, that is,

$$
E_t[r^{net}_{i,t+1}] = E_t[r_{i,t+1}] - E_t[TC]. \tag{3}
$$

We describe how we estimate transaction costs in the paragraph thereafter.

**Expected Returns.** We know from a rapidly growing literature that tools from machine learning are suitable to recover expected returns of individual stocks (e.g., Gu et al., 2020; Beckmeyer and Wiedemann, 2025) and other asset classes (e.g., Bali et al., 2023). Using these insights, we follow Gu et al. (2020) and express our estimate of future expected gross returns as

$$
E_t[r_{i,t+1}] = f(c_{i,t}). \tag{4}
$$

where $f(\cdot)$ is a flexible function mapping today's firm characteristics to expected future returns.[^3] As the authors show that neural networks outperform competing models in the task of stock return predictions, we follow their findings and use a feed-forward neural network with three hidden layers and the pyramidal layering scheme described in Gu et al. (2020) to estimate the mapping $f$. As the estimation of the neural network is computationally costly, we re-estimate the model on a yearly frequency. That is, we re-fit our machine learning estimator with information from up until the end of December each year and hold it fixed the entire next year. As our first out-of-sample period starts in January 2004, we use the preceding 23 years — starting in January 1980 — to estimate the first model. We use the first 70% of observations to train the model's parameters and validate the model in the remaining 30% of months. We estimate 50 trials and use an equal-weighted ensemble of the five best models for the final return prediction. For the predictions in 2005 we expand the training sample with the information from 2004 and re-estimate the model. We repeat the process until all years in our sample are exhausted.

[^3]: As before, we use the dataset with information on 153 distinct firm characteristics from Jensen et al. (2023) for this task.

In a first step we only include this gross return estimate in our double-sorting exercise. As our transaction cost estimates are additive, we can separately study their impact on the overall performance.

**Transaction Costs.** Of course, a fund looking to invest by characteristic $c$ will naturally also incorporate a notion of expected and realized transaction costs before allocating capital into a specific stock. For this component, we follow Jensen et al. (2025) and model the (expected and realized) dollar transaction costs of a trade with dollar-size vector $\tau_t$ as

$$
TC_{t,i} = \frac{1}{2} \tau_t' \Lambda_{i,t} \tau_t, \tag{5}
$$

where the factor of $\frac{1}{2}$ reflects the standard assumption that the realized cost is split equally between buyer and seller and $\Lambda_{i,t}$ is a diagonal matrix of price-impact coefficients, calibrated to the empirical estimates of Frazzini et al. (2018). Specifically, Frazzini et al. (2018) document that trading 1% of a stock's daily dollar volume moves its price by approximately 0.1%, implying a linear price-impact coefficient of $0.1\% / 1\% = 0.1$ per unit of daily dollar volume. Consequently,

$$
\Lambda_{i,t} = \frac{0.2}{V_{i,t}}, \tag{6}
$$

where $V_{i,t}$ is the expected daily dollar volume of stock $i$ on day $t$.[^4] Intuitively, stocks with higher turnover are cheaper to trade: a larger $V_{i,t}$ implies a smaller price-impact coefficient and therefore lower transaction costs for a given trade size. At the same time, the quadratic form in (5) implies that costs are convex in trade size, capturing the empirical regularity that large orders face increasing marginal price impact and therefore penalizing excessive concentration in illiquid stocks. Following Frazzini et al. (2018) and Jensen et al. (2025), we proxy for expected daily volume using the average daily dollar volume over the trailing six months.

[^4]: To see how (6) arises, note that a trade of size $\tau$ moves the price by $0.001 \times (\tau / 0.01 V_{i,t}) = 0.1 \tau / V_{i,t}$, so the dollar cost of the trade is $\tau \times 0.1 \tau / V_{i,t} = 0.1 \tau^2 / V_{i,t}$. Matching this to $\frac{1}{2} \Lambda_{i,t} \tau^2$ immediately gives $\Lambda_{i,t} = 0.2 / V_{i,t}$.

## 3. Reviving Anomalies

**Ex Post Analysis.** In the first step, we demonstrate the effectiveness of our machine learning augmentation of standard factor portfolios. For this reason, we abstract from expected transaction costs and focus on a double sort based on the respective factor and expected returns. We first show that augmenting characteristic-sorted high-minus-low portfolios with the expected returns of portfolio constituents severely improves their out-of-sample performance over the period from January 2004 to December 2023. In Figure 1, the top panel illustrates the average improvement in annualized gross returns, while the bottom panel reports the corresponding average $t$-statistic from a comparison of the standard HML return with the expected return–augmented HML return. We cluster characteristics by the themes identified by Jensen et al. (2023) and report average improvements within each theme.

We observe substantial economic and statistically significant improvements across all characteristic themes, ranging from +12.5% for characteristics capturing the seasonality of stock returns to +22% for size and low-risk characteristics. Notably, the average improvement within each of the 13 themes generates gross returns exceeding those of a market-wide investment over the same period, highlighting the benefits of incorporating stock-specific expected returns into systematic investment strategies. The $t$-statistics reported in the lower panel are consistently large, ranging from 2.3 for seasonality to over 6 for size. Many of these $t$-values exceed the elevated threshold of 3 recommended by Harvey, Liu, and Zhu (2015) to mitigate concerns of multiple hypothesis testing and $p$-hacking.

While the gross-of-fees improvements depicted in Figure 1 are great, they do not indicate whether an investor could have profited from such an investment, as they ignore transaction costs. Price impact costs for large institutions are known to increase with invested capital, which we acknowledge in the definition of transaction costs in Eq. (6). Institutional investors hold approximately half of the U.S. equity market (Gârleanu and Pedersen, 2022), and Edelen, Evans, and Kadlec (2007) show that two thirds of transaction costs for mutual funds are due to price impact costs, far outweighing proportional costs such as bid–ask spreads. We therefore examine how many of the 153 characteristic-based anomaly portfolios would have generated significant out-of-sample net returns for institutions with different AUM. We assume four terminal fund sizes in December 2023. These four levels are inspired by the distribution of fund sizes in the CRSP Mutual Fund Database, roughly corresponding to the 20th, 40th, 60th, and 80th percentiles. We consider only mutual funds with an equity share of at least 50% (based on holdings matched to our JKP sample) and a minimum of 10 matched stocks. The distribution of fund sizes is reported in Appendix A. Following Jensen et al. (2025), we discount fund sizes back in time using the market return, which approximately reflects the general growth of the industry.

Table 1 reports the results. The first panel (Unadjusted) presents the number of anomaly portfolios that generate significant net returns between 2004 and 2023 when stocks are sorted into quintiles by characteristic $c$. These portfolios are constructed without incorporating either expected returns or expected transaction costs. As expected, the number of significant anomalies decreases with the fund size. A micro fund managing 40mn USD has 17 characteristic-sorted anomaly portfolios at its disposal, a small (medium-sized) fund managing 200mn (500mn) USD has 10 (3), and a large fund managing 1.6bn USD has only a single anomaly available. The average portfolio returns are broadly comparable, ranging from 6–8% per year, with Sharpe ratios around 0.6, roughly matching the market over the same period. Maximum drawdowns are moderate, amounting to approximately one-third of the invested capital.

Adjusting factor exposures by the expected gross return component of the constituent stocks greatly amplifies the number of anomaly portfolios exhibiting *ex post* significant net returns for micro- to medium-sized funds. For micro funds, 126 of the 153 anomaly portfolios display significant net returns; for small funds, 73 portfolios do so; and for medium-sized funds, 9 portfolios. Only for the largest mutual funds in the CRSP Mutual Fund Database does a mere expected return adjustment fail to uncover additional investment opportunities. Indeed, not a single anomaly portfolio exhibits significant returns after incorporating price impact costs for a large fund. Average net returns decline slightly with fund size, ranging from 21% per year for micro funds to 16% for medium-sized funds. Realized net Sharpe ratios follow a similar pattern, decreasing from 0.82 to 0.57, as return standard deviations are broadly comparable. Notably, maximum drawdowns increase substantially after adjusting for the expected return component of the stocks, ranging between −55% and −60%, highlighting that investing in high-expected-return stocks intensifies the risk of "being wrong".

Once we augment the construction of anomaly portfolios by incorporating both the expected gross returns of the constituent stocks and their expected transaction costs, we observe a substantial increase in the number of viable investment portfolios for medium- to large-sized funds. While the number of significant anomalies is roughly comparable for micro and small funds, it rises sharply for medium-sized and large funds. Considering only expected returns identifies 9 (0) anomaly portfolios with significant net returns for a medium-sized (large) fund. Including expected transaction costs increases these numbers to 81 (41) anomaly portfolios. Average net returns are 18% per year for a micro fund, decreasing to 9% per year for a large fund. The standard deviation of returns declines with fund size, resulting in realized net Sharpe ratios that are broadly comparable across micro to medium-sized funds, at 0.75 and 0.68, respectively. Interestingly, maximum drawdowns are also lower than when augmenting only by expected returns, suggesting that several investment opportunities that appear promising on paper are not investable for medium-sized to large funds.

## 4. Mutual Funds

We have proposed two simple augmentations that vastly increase the number of implementable anomaly portfolios for funds of all sizes. In this section, we examine whether funds incorporate information regarding which anomalies are *ex ante* implementable into their investment decisions. Specifically, we demonstrate that funds strategically tilt their portfolios toward anomalies that are more likely to be implementable net of price impact costs, conditional on their AUM. We formalize this behavior through a new measure, **anomaly alignment**.

**Anomaly Alignment.** We merge the CRSP Mutual Fund Database with the 153 firm-level characteristics documented in Jensen et al. (2023), which are constructed using information from CRSP and Compustat. To each applicable holding, we add information about the 153 firm-level characteristics. Every month, we calculate the funds' AUM and their respective equity share. Since we are investigating the profitability of equity anomaly portfolios, we restrict the sample to funds with an equity share of at least 50% based on holdings matched to our JKP dataset, and with at least 10 matched stocks.

We define the anomaly alignment of fund $f$ with respect to each anomaly $a$ at time $t$ as follows. For each anomaly and point in time, we construct a target portfolio that jointly incorporates expected return forecasts from our machine learning model and expected transaction costs estimated via the quadratic price impact model. Following prior methodology, the target portfolio is obtained through a double sort on the respective anomaly and expected returns net of transaction costs. Because transaction costs are a function of fund size, we compute target portfolios over a grid of 100 fund sizes, linearly spaced according to the empirical distribution of fund sizes in the sample (see Figure A). For computational efficiency, each fund is then assigned the target portfolio corresponding to the nearest grid size. Within each target portfolio, each stock $i$ is assigned a dummy variable $D_{i,a,t}$ which equals 1 if the stock is included in the long leg, −1 if in the short leg, and 0 otherwise. The fund's anomaly alignment with respect to anomaly $a$ is then computed as the sum of the portfolio weights $w_{f,i,t}$ multiplied by the corresponding dummy values, based on the fund's actual holdings at time $t$:

$$
\text{Anomaly Alignment}_{f,a,t} = \sum_i w_{f,i,t} \cdot D_{i,a,t}. \tag{7}
$$

Economically, a higher anomaly alignment indicates that a fund more closely tracks our augmented anomaly portfolio.

**Proof of Principle.** Using the previous double sort, we estimate which of the 153 anomaly portfolios are likely implementable net of fees, taking into account both expected returns and anticipated transaction costs. At each point in time, each fund has 153 different anomaly alignments, which are classified as either implementable or non-implementable. In a final step, we compute the mean anomaly alignment across anomalies, distinguishing between those identified as implementable and those classified as non-implementable.

To examine whether mutual funds actively tilt their portfolios toward implementable anomalies, we estimate the following panel regression:

$$
\text{Anomaly Alignment}_{f,t} = \mathbf{1}_{f,t} + TE + FE, \tag{8}
$$

where $\mathbf{1}_{f,t}$ is an indicator variable equal to 1 when the anomaly alignment corresponds to the mean of implementable anomalies, and 0 when it corresponds to the mean of non-implementable anomalies. $TE$ and $FE$ denote time- and fund-fixed effects, respectively. Standard errors are clustered by time and entity.

The results in Table 4 indicate that funds align their holdings towards implementable anomalies and away from anomalies unachievable to them. Because the selection of investable anomalies depends on fund size, this approach implicitly accounts for differences in AUM. On average, the anomaly alignment for implementable anomalies is 0.05 higher than for non-implementable anomalies without fixed effects. Incorporating time-fixed effects increases the difference to 0.11, while including both time- and fund-fixed effects further raises it to 0.12. Overall, the findings provide clear evidence that mutual funds systematically align their portfolios with implementable anomalies.

**Regression Setting.** To examine whether fund characteristics are influenced by anomaly alignment, we use regression analyses. Consistent with the previous analysis, we distinguish between the mean anomaly alignment of implementable and non-implementable anomalies. This allows us to assess whether changes in the anomaly alignment of implementable anomalies have a different effect on fund characteristics compared with changes in the anomaly alignment of non-implementable anomalies. We begin with cross-sectional regressions in which the mean of a fund characteristic is regressed on the fund's mean anomaly alignment:

$$
\text{Fund Characteristic}_f = \text{Intercept} + \text{Anomaly Alignment}_f + \text{Controls}, \tag{9}
$$

where $\text{Fund Characteristic}_f$ represents an average attribute of fund $f$ (e.g., fund returns or management fees) over time, while $\text{Anomaly Alignment}_f$ denotes the fund's average anomaly alignment across anomalies and time. We estimate two separate regressions: one for implementable anomalies and one for non-implementable anomalies. Standard errors are heteroskedasticity-robust (HC3). These cross-sectional regressions provide insights into how average fund characteristics relate to the average anomaly alignment.

Next, we extend this analysis using panel regressions to capture the time-varying nature of anomaly alignment:

$$
\text{Fund Characteristic}_{f,t} = \text{Anomaly Alignment}_{f,t-1} + TE + FE + \text{Controls}, \tag{10}
$$

where $\text{Fund Characteristic}_{f,t}$ denotes a specific attribute of fund $f$ at time $t$, and $\text{Anomaly Alignment}_{f,t}$ represents the average anomaly alignment across anomalies for fund $f$ at time $t-1$. Analogously, we estimate two separate regressions, one for implementable anomalies and one for non-implementable anomalies. Standard errors are clustered at the fund and time levels. This panel regression framework enables us to assess how anomaly alignment predicts future fund characteristics.

Theoretically, our measure is unbounded. Therefore, we rely on its empirical distribution to provide an economic interpretation of the regression coefficients. In the full sample, the mean anomaly alignment across both implementable and non-implementable anomalies is 0.01, with a standard deviation of 0.10. The minimum and maximum values of the mean anomaly alignment are −0.36 and 0.57, respectively, and the 25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles are −0.05, 0.02, and 0.07. To evaluate the economic significance of the regression coefficients, we use its IQR of 0.11. Summary statistics for mutual fund characteristics are reported in Appendix B.

**Fund Returns.** As a first fund characteristic, we examine monthly returns to assess the impact of anomaly alignment on fund performance. Specifically, we investigate how monthly returns are affected when fund managers indirectly follow our approach by implementing anomaly portfolios that account for expected returns and transaction costs. In Table 5, we regress the mean fund return on the mean anomaly alignment for both implementable and non-implementable anomalies. For implementable anomalies, the results indicate that anomaly alignment has a significantly positive effect on fund returns. Without controls, a one IQR increase in anomaly alignment raises the average monthly return by 0.62 percentage points, increasing to 0.71 percentage points after including controls. This effect is economically meaningful as it translates into a difference of 7.39 and 8.48 percentage points in annualized returns, respectively.[^5] Thus, funds that indirectly invest in implementable anomalies by accounting for expected returns and transaction costs achieve significantly higher returns on average. In contrast, investments in non-implementable anomalies do not produce a significant effect on average returns. These findings highlight the importance of both expected returns and transaction costs, as positions in non-implementable anomalies do not translate into higher realized returns.

## 6. Conclusion

In this paper, we introduce a novel framework to construct anomaly portfolios that jointly incorporates machine learning return forecasts and scale-dependent price impacts. Our approach addresses the prevailing view that most anomalies become insignificant once implementation costs are taken into account by showing that a large set of anomalies can be revived across a wide range of fund sizes. We find that expected returns are more important for anomaly portfolios of smaller funds, whereas expected transaction costs are especially relevant for larger funds. Across anomaly themes, our framework performs particularly well for quality- and accrual-based strategies. In contrast, size-based anomalies cannot be revived for most fund sizes, consistent with their reliance on illiquid, small-cap stocks. Importantly, because our method operates as an overlay on standard factor portfolios, it preserves their interpretability while selecting only the most promising investment opportunities within each factor portfolio. Investors seeking to exploit return anomalies can therefore readily incorporate our framework into existing strategies.

Building on this framework, we introduce the concept of implementable anomalies, defined as anomalies that are expected to generate positive net returns conditional on fund size and time. A simple 1/N investment strategy in implementable anomalies delivers economically meaningful out-of-sample Sharpe ratios ranging from 0.64 to 1.01 across fund sizes. The resulting portfolios exhibit negative market exposure ($\beta < 0$) and generate large and significant alphas, implying substantial diversification benefits relative to a long-only market investment. We leave the question of optimal portfolio allocation between implementable anomalies and a long-only market investment to future research.

Finally, we provide evidence that mutual funds behave in a manner consistent with our framework, as they tilt their holdings toward implementable anomalies and away from non-implementable ones. Mutual funds with higher exposure to implementable anomalies earn significantly higher net returns, in line with the economic intuition of our framework. In contrast, exposure to non-implementable anomalies is associated with lower management fees, suggesting that investors recognize the distinction between implementable and non-implementable anomalies for a given mutual fund. Overall, these findings indicate that our framework captures salient features of institutional investment behavior and serves as a practical tool for identifying implementable return anomalies across different fund sizes.
